## The One-Sentence Version

**You win high-friction deals by making the buyer feel safe faster than their urgency fades — but only if the deal is complex enough that your competitors can't copy your homework.**

---

## The Four Ideas

### 1. Surplus, Not Value

Most sales training teaches you to "sell value." ILG teaches you to **create surplus**.

What's the difference? Value is what your product does. Surplus is what's *left over* after the buyer subtracts everything it costs them to change — the internal politics, the implementation risk, the switching costs, the six months of parallel workflows, the career risk if it goes sideways.

A deal with massive value and massive friction has zero surplus. It doesn't close.

Here's the counterintuitive part: in complex deals, you create more surplus by reducing friction than by increasing value. Every enterprise buyer has heard the value pitch. They've seen the ROI calculator. They know the product is good. What they don't know is whether it'll actually *work* in their specific environment without blowing up. That uncertainty is the friction, and it's the thing standing between you and the deal.

Also — and this is important — the buyer isn't comparing you to another vendor. In most Bridge deals, the real alternative is building in-house, extending a workaround, reallocating the budget to a different problem, or just staying out of the market entirely. After a failed implementation, buyers don't go find vendor #2 — they retreat from the category. If you don't know what the buyer's real alternatives are, you're flying blind.

### 2. The Fear Multiplier

Friction has two components: the real cost of change, and the *amplified* cost created by what each side doesn't know.

This is bilateral — it goes both ways:

**When you don't understand their world** (seller ignorance), you scope wrong, demo wrong, and propose things that don't fit. The buyer sees this and thinks: "They don't get it. This is going to be a mess." Your credibility drops. Their fear goes up.

**When they don't understand your solution** (buyer uncertainty), they imagine worst-case scenarios. They've been burned before. They add buffer time, extra committee reviews, and "just one more question" cycles. Every unknown becomes a reason to delay.

These two gaps create *different* problems and need *different* solutions. You fix seller ignorance with discovery — the Blueprint, workflow mapping, asking questions nobody else asks. You fix buyer uncertainty with costly signals — workshops, technical audits, paid pilots, anything that says "we're investing real resources because we're confident this works."

The key insight: figure out which gap is wider and close that one first.

### 3. The Decay Clock

The thing that created the buying window — a failed audit, a regulatory deadline, a board mandate, a lost customer — has a shelf life. Every week you spend in the sales cycle, that urgency fades a little.

The pain becomes less acute. Workarounds start to feel permanent. Budget gets redirected. The champion who brought you in moves to a different priority or a different company.

This means you're in a race, but not against the competition. You're racing against time. You need to make the buyer feel safe (close the fear multiplier) *before* the value of solving their problem decays below the threshold where the deal is worth doing.

This is why ILG front-loads the heavy work. The Blueprint happens early, not because it's "process" but because every day of delay makes the deal less likely — not from competition, but from organizational entropy.

**One important exception:** new economic events can restart the clock. A second failed audit, a competitor going live, a new CIO with a mandate — these reset the urgency. If you've already done discovery work on a stalled deal and a fresh trigger fires, that's often the best moment to re-engage. Your homework is still valid, but their pain is renewed.

The worst trap: when urgency is high but the buyer doesn't have their own process figured out (we call this the Chaos Trap). The temptation is to rush. But rushing increases the fear multiplier, which can kill the deal even when the need is real. Better to pause, get the foundation right, and move fast from a solid base.

### 4. The Boundary Condition (Know When NOT to Use ILG)

ILG is expensive. Blueprints, Red Teams, and MIPs require real investment from both sides. That investment only pays off when two things are true:

**The deal is complex enough.** If the buyer can set up your product in an afternoon without talking to anyone, they don't need a Blueprint. They need a free trial and good docs. ILG is for deals where the product touches workflows, requires integration, changes how people do their jobs, or has real implementation risk.

**The market is thin enough.** If there are 50 vendors who all do the same thing, your Blueprint becomes table stakes — everyone can do one. ILG differentiates when there are only a handful of vendors who can credibly serve the buyer's specific need. That's when your investment in understanding their world is hard to replicate.

**Watch for boundary drift.** Both complexity and market thickness change over time. Standards mature, APIs open, and SaaS vendors enter to universalize workflows that previously required custom builds. A Bridge deal today can become a Toaster next year. Before investing in ILG, ask not just "is this complex enough now?" but "will it stay complex through the contract term?"

Simple test before investing in ILG for any deal: "Is this specific enough that a generic pitch will fail? And are there few enough competitors that our discovery work actually sets us apart?"

If both answers are yes, ILG. If not, PLG or SLG will get you there faster and cheaper.

## How They Connect

Think of it as a decision chain:

1. **Check the boundary** → Is this deal complex enough and the market thin enough for ILG?
2. **Map the surplus** → What's the value gap versus the friction? Is there room for a deal?
3. **Diagnose the fear** → Which side has the bigger knowledge gap? Fix that one first.
4. **Watch the clock** → How fast is urgency fading? Compress the cycle accordingly.

The artifacts (Blueprint → Red Team → MIP) map directly to this chain. The Blueprint maps friction and closes seller ignorance. The Red Team stress-tests the plan and closes buyer uncertainty. The MIP locks in the surplus before the clock runs out.

## The Old Equation vs. The New One

**Old (v9):** "The probability of a sale is driven by risk minimization, not value maximization."

**New (v10):** "In high-specificity deals, reducing friction creates more surplus than increasing value. But friction is amplified by information gaps and value erodes with time — so the goal is to close the asymmetry gap faster than urgency decays."

The shift: we went from a single claim about risk vs. value to a *story* with four moving parts. It's more complex, but it actually tells the rep what to *do* — not just what to believe.
