## Section 3: The Economics — Why This Is a Market for Lemons [STITCH + BRAD REVIEWS]
**Purpose:** Apply Akerlof's "Market for Lemons" framework. This is where you bring the economic theory in, same move as the TCE blog post.

**The argument:**
1. **Akerlof's insight (1970):** When buyers can't assess quality before purchase, information asymmetry causes market failure. Sellers of high-quality goods can't differentiate themselves, so the market collapses toward mediocrity.
2. **Spence's signaling theory (1973):** Credible signals require *cost* to send. If anyone can claim "best AI" at zero cost, the claim carries zero information. It's a cheap signal.
3. **The HIMSS floor is a market for lemons.** Every booth makes the same claims → buyers can't distinguish quality from noise → they stop trying to evaluate through the market mechanism entirely.
4. **AI has accelerated this.** Vibe coding has lowered the barrier to spinning up products, flooding the market with solutions that may address a surface-level workflow issue but don't solve fundamental business problems — and may actually add future complexity and fragility.

**Research ammunition:**
- Akerlof: information asymmetry → market failure → buyers distrust all sellers equally
- Spence: credible signals require cost; free claims are meaningless
- AI commoditization research: "best AI" targets the commodity layer; real moats are data + integration
- 73% of B2B executives rank peer recommendations as most influential (buyers have already abandoned cheap signals)

## Section 4: The "Easy Integration" Trap — An Anti-Credibility Signal [BRAD WRITES — story beats locked]
**Purpose:** This is the sharpest, most ILG-specific insight in the piece. Promising easy implementation is not just wrong — it's a signal that you don't understand the buyer's world.

**Story beats (from interview — March 2026):**

**Beat 1 — The dismissal.** Saying integration will be easy is dismissive of the complex business workflows and hundreds of hours of meetings those people have sat through. Even if it's true that you have a simple or quick integration, it's potentially calling them stupid for not figuring out how to solve the problem earlier — or it's an indication that you're not solving a hard problem.

**Beat 2 — The double bind.** You're either telling the buyer they should have figured this out already, or you're telling them your solution doesn't go deep enough to matter. Neither builds trust.

**Beat 3 — The scheduling example (no vendors named).** One big area of investment: voice agents automating patient outreach — scheduling follow-ups, collecting pre-visit information. I'll save my rant for why phone calls are a terrible UX for another time, but there is clear efficacy in automating this. Computers can make more phone calls than a person. Scheduling is HARD, and it's great that companies want to help fill schedules. A fuller schedule impacts the top line. But there's very little stopping me from spinning up Claude Code and copying your business, because AI phone calls are a solved problem. You know what's NOT a solved problem? Developing a staffing and hiring forecasting model based on new utilization. Understanding potential hospitalization needs for those visits. Ensuring you have the right cleaning staff and autoclave capacity for your now-full surgery schedule. I'm not saying integrating with scheduling systems is easy — it's not (reference Brendan's old article) — but filling a provider's schedule barely scratches the surface on the operational impact of your service.

**Beat 4 — The reframe.** Talk about hard problems and talk about the hard work of solving hard problems. Technical integration is one step of the organizational change effort. Customer discovery is your friend.

**Beat 5 — The radiology example.** Consider a vendor deploying AI to help radiologists diagnose images faster. Great — but faster means the radiologist is now skipping a cognitive step they used to perform manually. If the AI goes away or is wrong, the radiologist is actually *worse* than before. The deep-thinking vendor recognizes that and builds a training program — not just to maintain performance, but to increase the radiologist's capability with *or without* the technology. That's not "easy integration." That's thinking deeply about what happens after the integration.

**Beat 6 — The consequence.** Ignoring these factors leads to low asset specificity. You haven't tied your success to the customer's success. You're easy to dump.

**Important note:** No specific vendors are named in this section. All examples reference product categories and AI capabilities generically. This keeps the argument about the *pattern*, not any individual company. Specific vendor analysis with names and data comes in Section 6 when we zoom into the ambient scribe market.

**The TCE connection (I'll stitch in):** Claiming "easy implementation" is telling a buyer who lives in a world of high asset specificity, bounded rationality, and prior hold-up experiences that you don't understand any of those things. It's the opposite of the ILG playbook — instead of demonstrating deep contextual knowledge, you're broadcasting ignorance.

**Research support:**

## Section 5: Where Buyers Go Instead — The TCE Decision [STITCH + BRAD WRITES]
**Purpose:** When the market mechanism fails, buyers face a TCE decision about whether to use the market at all. Three outcomes, all bad for vendors with cheap signals.

**Key points (from Brad's interview):**
- "It would be difficult to justify evaluating a solution based on its marketing language"
- "This is why we build technical integration guides" — costly, credible signals
- "It creates a non-market based pathway for growth — you buy from people you know and trust, rather than relying on a good product"

**The three directions (Brad's insight + research):**

1. **FOMU — They don't buy at all.** The "no decision" outcome. When buyers can't differentiate through the market, the rational response is to not participate. This maps directly to the 40-60% no-decision rate from the TCE blog.

2. **The big boys keep winning.** By reducing their value proposition to a technology ("best AI for X"), vendors are inadvertently commoditizing themselves. They're telling the buyer the differentiation is in the AI layer — which is exactly the layer incumbents can absorb as a rounding error. Epic just ships the feature. The vendor marketing millions at HIMSS are effectively making a market for *less differentiated foundational AI models*. You're spending money to prove you don't matter.
   - **Live example:** KLAS just released its Ambient Speech rankings — Abridge (#1), Ambience (#2), Suki (#3)... and then Epic announced its own ambient speech offering. KLAS reports that near-term adoption is being driven by "vendor loyalty and consolidation benefits" and the long-term outlook favors Epic exclusivity. The companies that marketed on "best AI for documentation" just watched Epic turn their moat claim into a feature checkbox.

3. **They build it themselves — the TCE nuclear option.** When transaction costs of using the market get high enough, the firm brings the activity in-house. Williamson literally predicted this. Stanford Health Care built ChatEHR — their own AI-powered EHR interface — because point solutions had become unmanageable. Nigam Shah, Stanford's chief data scientist: the health system already runs ~1,500 IT systems, and every new application brings "its own integration challenges, identity and access management, bug fixes, updates... It is utter madness." By early 2026, 1,450 clinicians were using ChatEHR. Stanford didn't need the "best AI" — they needed fewer vendors.
   - This is vertical integration by the buyer. The most sophisticated health systems aren't going to HIMSS to find their AI partner — they're building it in-house because the market's transaction costs (integration tax, vendor management overhead, co-innovation demands) exceeded the value of the point solution.

**The regression irony:** In a market that should be evolving toward ILG, bad marketing is pushing buyers backward in three directions — to no-decision paralysis, to the incumbent's bundled offering, or to bypassing the vendor market entirely. The vendors investing millions in marketing booths are driving their buyers toward purchasing models where the booth is irrelevant.

**Research support:**
- 80% of B2B buying journey happens independently of vendor contact
- 90% of B2B buyers say peer recommendations influence their decisions
- Buyers spending 11+ months on evaluation not because they want to, but because vendor claims force deeper verification
- Stanford ChatEHR: built in-house starting 2023, piloted 2025, 1,450 clinicians by Jan 2026
- Epic Ambient Speech Announcement: customers welcome it, long-term outlook favors exclusivity (KLAS 2026)

### Confirming
- **Akerlof, "The Market for Lemons" (1970)** — Information asymmetry causes market failure; directly supports your cheap signals argument
- **Spence, "Job Market Signaling" (1973)** — Credible signals require cost; free claims carry zero information
