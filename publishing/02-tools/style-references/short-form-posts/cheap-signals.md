This morning's HTN newsletter had me looking at some care delivery websites and thinking about the [transaction] cost of cheap messaging. Most of us think of marketing as reducing friction - helping buyers find and trust you faster. But the wrong message does the opposite.

Economist George Akerlof won a Nobel Prize for describing markets where buyers can't verify quality before purchase. He called it the "Market for Lemons." When sellers make claims that cost nothing to make, buyers can't tell who's real. So they assume the worst.

Healthcare is the ultimate lemon market. Most signals are cheap. "Patient-centered care." "We treat you like family." Any practice can say this tomorrow - the best and the worst. When everyone makes the same claim, the claim carries no information.

Cheap signals don't just fail to build trust. They actively increase the buyer's work. Now I have to dig deeper to figure out if you're legitimate. That's friction. That increases the buyer's search and consensus costs.

Costly signals work differently. These are commitments that are hard to fake because they require sacrifice. for care delivery companies, this can look like publishing your patient panel cap or posting transparent pricing.

When a DPC practice says "I cap my panel at 600 patients," they're signalling that they are leaving money on the table. That's credible. When they say "we provide the best care ever," that's noise.

The lesson for anyone marketing a service where quality is hard to verify upfront: your business model is your best marketing asset. Structural commitments communicate more than taglines ever will.

Lead with what you've given up in order to provide exceptional service, not with what you can hypothetically promise.

The Zero Interest Rate era taught a generation of founders a dangerous lie: that all friction is bad.

We were told every user flow had to be seamless, every onboarding instant, every barrier removed. "Growth at all costs" meant eliminating anything that slowed the user down.

But we confused friction with waste. They're not the same thing.

Friction can be the filter that ensures quality. Two examples from completely different worlds:

In healthcare:

The DPC membership fee is financial friction. It prevents the churn-and-burn of volume medicine. It forces the patient to commit, and it forces the doctor to deliver value to keep them.

In social media:

Front Porch Forum in Vermont became one of the most successful local networks in America by doing the opposite of what every platform "expert" would tell you to do. They introduced temporal friction.

FPF doesn't let you post instantly. They use a once-a-day digest model with proactive moderation. If you want to yell at your neighbor about their lawn signs, you have to write it, wait for the digest, and sit with the fact that your whole town will read it tomorrow.

That 24-hour delay cools the temperature. It replaces engagement with civility. It's why the vast majority of FPF users say the platform is valuable to their community - while the "frictionless" platforms optimized themselves into anxiety machines.

The lesson:

If you only want scale, remove friction.

If you want trust, insert friction.

The strongest communities - whether a DPC practice or a local network - are built behind a gate, not an open door.
