# Unforced Errors on the Conference Floor
### There's no way generic marketing messages are working, right?
#### March 2026

---

## The Zoo

All industry conferences are zoos, and walking into HIMSS or ViVE is no different. Booths stacked on top of each other, the only way to find somebody is to look up and triangulate with the hanging brand signs. Bright colors, pithy messages: "We're doing it! And we're doing it with AI!" One guy was walking around with a fake, gelatinous hand on his head — fun, attention-grabbing, though I genuinely don't know what they were selling. That's the point. It looks good on LinkedIn, but is it closing deals? And it feels overwhelming, not just because you can still hear the buzz of the fluorescent lights when you get back to the room and are trying to fall asleep after that espresso martini.

I'm not even a buyer, and I find it exhausting. Now imagine being a hospital CIO, already managing hundreds of vendor relationships, or a CFO trying to figure out which of those relationships are actually driving performance. Put yourself in that headspace for a second. You walk the floor and pass fifteen point solution vendors with the "first ever agentic coding solution" — weird that fifteen of them were the first, no? — each promising a one-click integration. Would you stop at their booth? Would you spend a moment considering them?

My gut says no. So do the numbers — [80% of the B2B buying journey now happens before a buyer ever talks to a vendor](https://bthorson.substack.com/p/transaction-cost-economics-the-evolution), and [94% of buying groups have already ranked their preferred vendors before initiating first contact](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/buyer-experience-report-2025/). When a buyer can't distinguish signal from noise, their options narrow fast: do nothing, buy from someone they already know, or build it themselves.

So why does anyone show up? The most sought-after buyers — the CIOs, the VPs of IT, the clinical informatics leads — come for a handful of pre-scheduled meetings with vendors they already know. The conference uses the convenience of those few anchor meetings to entice the rest of us with a chance to pitch the big dogs. And based on what I saw, many of us are setting a trap for ourselves.

## The Trap

So what is the trap, exactly? Try telling one booth apart from the next. Everyone has the best AI. Everyone offers seamless integration. Everyone is transforming healthcare. At some point the superlatives stop functioning as language and start functioning as wallpaper.

This is a classic signal-to-noise problem, and it has corrosive effects on your ability to compete.

When everyone says they're the best, buyers are going to have to rely on some other function to evaluate credibility. And it's not just "we're the best" — product marketing loves to cite the metric they beat the competition on. I imagine that when a vendor says they're the best at summarization speed or identifying ICD-10 codes or making doctors happy, they think they're making it easier for a buyer with that particular concern to select their tool. In reality, they're turning an apples-to-oranges comparison into an apples-to-horseshoes comparison — and the buyer doesn't need apples or horseshoes.

For enterprise buyers — the ones who've been through a failed implementation and know what's at stake — vanity metrics don't just miss the mark. They actively erode trust. These buyers want to know you understand their fears and how you'll address them.

So the buyer hears "best AI" for the fifteenth time, and they stop listening. Not because they're rude — because they're rational. You've given them no mechanism to distinguish you from the fourteen booths they already walked past.

Minimizing complexity in your marketing is actively hurting your growth:

- [67% of developers abandon tools after discovering hidden costs not disclosed during evaluation](https://www.softwareseni.com/why-ai-coding-speed-gains-disappear-in-code-reviews/) (Harness, State of Software Delivery 2025)
- [Half of enterprise deal failures stem from failure to meet technical requirements during needs assessment](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2024/most-large-scale-tech-programs-fail-how-to-succeed) (BCG, 2024)
- [Only 9% of buyers consider vendor website claims to be reliable sources](https://corporatevisions.com/blog/b2b-buying-behavior-statistics-trends/) (G2, 2024)

The buyer who's lived through a failed implementation — who knows from painful experience that nothing about health tech integration is easy or seamless — now has a data point about you: you either don't understand their world, or you're not being honest about it. Once a potential buyer discovers that gap between your marketing and their reality, credibility is destroyed and nearly impossible to regain.

Your marketing isn't just ineffective. It's working against you.

## The Market for Lemons

There's a name for what's happening on the conference floor. Economist George Akerlof described it in 1970 in a paper about used cars.

Akerlof's insight was deceptively simple: when buyers can't assess quality before purchase, the market breaks. Buyers know that some cars are good and some are lemons, but they can't tell which is which before they buy. So they offer a price that reflects the *average* quality in the market. Sellers of genuinely good cars can't get fair value, so they leave. The market spirals toward mediocrity — and eventually, only lemons remain.

The HIMSS floor has the same structure. Dozens of vendors in any given category, all making functionally identical claims. The buyer can't verify "best AI" or "seamless integration" without months of technical evaluation. So they do what Akerlof's buyers do — they either discount everyone equally, or they stop participating in the market altogether. I wish conference organizers did more to prevent their showroom floors from turning into used car lots with those inflatable tube men out front, but alas, I'm not a conference organizer...until Brendan and I start Interop Con ;)

And the vendors relying on cheap signals aren't just failing to differentiate — they're polluting the information environment for everyone. Every "best AI" banner makes it harder for the vendor next door — the one who actually has the implementation data — to be heard. The barrier to standing up a product that *looks* credible from a booth has collapsed. A competent team can ship a working prototype in weeks that would have taken months two years ago. When a buyer asks "was that just an LLM wrapper?" and nobody on the floor can give a convincing answer that it wasn't, you have a market for lemons.

So how do high-quality vendors escape this? Michael Spence extended Akerlof's work a few years later with signaling theory. In a market plagued by information asymmetry, high-quality sellers differentiate themselves through *costly signals* — actions that are expensive to fake. A college degree is a costly signal because it requires years of effort. Claiming to be smart on a resume is a cheap signal because anyone can do it.

If you're still reading, you must agree with me on some level that "Best" is a cheap signal. It costs nothing to print on a banner, and it's also easier than ever to find some metric that competitors aren't focusing on and try to center the market around that. But costly signals aren't more expensive banners — they actively convey a massive investment the company had to make in order to print the message. Doing the hard work of implementation — and being able to talk about it specifically — is itself the costly signal. It gives you real reference points, real customers who can vouch for you, and real specificity that a competitor can't fake.

And buyers are looking for exactly that.

> 73% of B2B executives rank peer recommendations as the most influential factor in purchasing decisions. ([Wynter, 2024](https://wynter.com/post/how-b2b-saas-marketing-leaders-buy-2024))

That's not a preference statement — it's a rational adaptation to a broken market. When Iyengar and Lepper put 24 jam flavors on a table instead of 6, purchases dropped by a factor of ten — not because the jams got worse, but because the buyers couldn't tell them apart. Now scale that to a conference floor with dozens of ambient AI vendors.

Ambience Healthcare is thoughtful about this. It's not just that they reduced the documentation burden by 86% — it's equally or more important that they've already done it at 200 hospitals across dozens of specialties. It's not just that they're integrated — it's that they describe exactly how they integrate with each EHR, down to the specific API pathways and marketplace programs. That level of detail is the costly signal — you can't fake it without having built it. The buyers aren't confused. They've adapted. I think the vendors printing "best AI" on their banners just haven't caught up yet — which is pretty obvious, because I'm writing this blog.

## The "Easy Integration" Trap

There's another phrase that gets thrown around almost as much as "best AI," and it might be doing more damage: "easy integration."

Saying integration will be easy is dismissive of the complex business workflows and hundreds of hours of meetings those people have sat through. Think about it from the buyer's side. They've been living in implementation hell for years. They know what it takes to get a new system running inside a health system — the governance meetings, the IT reviews, the clinical workflow redesigns, the training, the go-live firefighting. They've earned that knowledge painfully. And now you're standing at a booth telling them it's easy? You're either saying they should have figured this out already — which isn't a great way to start a relationship — or you're telling them your solution doesn't go deep enough to touch the hard parts. Neither builds trust.

I'll give you an example. One big area of investment right now is voice agents automating patient outreach — scheduling follow-ups, collecting pre-visit information. I'll save my rant for why phone calls are a terrible UX for another time, but there's clear efficacy in automating this. Computers can make more phone calls than a person. Scheduling is hard, and it's great that companies want to help fill schedules. A fuller schedule impacts the top line.

But there's very little stopping me from spinning up Claude Code and copying your business, because AI phone calls are a solved problem. You know what's *not* a solved problem? Developing a staffing and hiring forecasting model based on new utilization. Understanding potential hospitalization needs for those visits. Making sure you have the right cleaning staff and autoclave capacity for your now-full surgery schedule. I'm not saying integrating with scheduling systems is easy — it's not — but filling a provider's schedule barely scratches the surface of the operational impact of your service.

Claiming "easy implementation" is telling a buyer who lives in a world of high asset specificity (deep, hard-to-unwind vendor relationships), bounded rationality (too many options, not enough information), and prior hold-up experiences (getting locked in by the last vendor who promised easy) that you don't understand any of those things. Every buyer has a scar from a vendor who promised easy and delivered a year of rework. You're not just making an inaccurate claim — you're triggering their worst memory. And once that gap between your marketing and their reality surfaces during technical evaluation, trust doesn't erode gradually. It collapses.

So talk about hard problems. Talk about the hard work of solving hard problems. Technical integration is one step of the organizational change effort, and customer discovery is your friend.

Consider a vendor deploying AI to help radiologists diagnose images faster. Great — but faster means the radiologist is now skipping a cognitive step they used to perform manually. If the AI is wrong, or if it goes away, the radiologist is actually *worse* than before. This isn't hypothetical — [a study out of Harvard Medical School](https://hms.harvard.edu/news/does-ai-help-or-hurt-human-radiologists-performance-depends-doctor) tracked 140 radiologists across 324 patient cases and found that AI assistance "did not uniformly improve diagnostic accuracy, and could even hurt performance." Experience level, specialization, prior AI exposure — none of it reliably predicted whether the tool would help or harm a given doctor's judgment. The deep-thinking vendor recognizes that and builds a training program — not just to maintain performance, but to increase the radiologist's capability with *or without* the technology. That's not "easy integration." That's the kind of depth that makes you hard to replace.

Skip it, and you're easy to dump.

## Where Buyers Go Instead

OK, so the market mechanism is broken. Buyers can't distinguish quality from noise. What do they actually do?

Academics have been studying this question for decades, and they've been remarkably consistent in their predictions. Iyengar and Lepper showed that too many options cause buyers to stop buying. Janis and Mann documented "defensive avoidance" — when no option looks safe, people defer, delegate, or double down on the status quo. Williamson predicted that when market transaction costs exceed the benefit, firms bring the activity in-house. Shapiro and Varian mapped how switching costs create incumbent lock-in independent of product quality.

These aren't health tech papers. They're about jam, used cars, and factory production. But here's what's happening in healthcare right now — and if you're a vendor relying on conference marketing to drive growth, the pattern match should concern you.

### Direction 1: They don't buy at all.

The polite version is "no decision." The honest version is the buyer decided you weren't worth the risk.

Look, when a CIO can't differentiate between fifteen vendors making the same claims, the rational move isn't to pick one and hope — it's to defer. That [40-60% no-decision rate](https://challengerinc.com/losing-to-customer-indecision/) in B2B enterprise sales? That's not a failure of sales technique. That's a market signal. Buyers are telling you: the information you gave me wasn't sufficient to justify the risk of choosing wrong.

[Matt Dixon and Ted McKenna](https://stevebizblog.com/new-research-shows-most-buyer-indecision-is-the-result-of-fear-of-messing-up/) studied 2.5 million sales calls and landed on a term for this: FOMU — the Fear of Messing Up. Not the fear of missing out on your product, but the fear that choosing any vendor in a market this opaque will end in a failed implementation, a blown budget, and a career-limiting conversation with the board. In that calculus, doing nothing is the safest option. And your booth full of superlatives just made that calculation easier.

### Direction 2: The incumbent absorbs your feature.

This is the existential one. When you reduce your value proposition to a technology — "best AI for X" — you're telling the buyer that the differentiation lives in the AI layer. But the AI layer is exactly what incumbents can absorb as a rounding error.

This isn't theoretical. [KLAS released its Ambient Speech rankings](https://klasresearch.com/best-in-klas-ranking/ambient-speech/2026/487) this year with Abridge, Ambience, and Suki leading the category. Then Epic announced its own ambient speech offering. [KLAS reports](https://hitconsultant.net/2025/09/29/klas-report-epics-native-ambient-speech-tool-reshapes-customer-ai-strategies/) that near-term adoption is being driven by vendor loyalty and consolidation benefits, and the long-term outlook favors Epic exclusivity.

The long-term outlook favors the incumbent bundling the feature. Let that sink in.

For any vendor whose entire pitch was "best AI for documentation," Epic just turned their moat claim into a feature checkbox. If you marketed on the AI layer — the commodity layer — you made the case for your own displacement. You spent money proving the feature was valuable enough that the platform should just include it. Not every ambient vendor fell into this trap — some built deep enough that the feature-vs-platform framing doesn't apply to them — but the ones who led with superlatives instead of implementation depth are watching the ground shift.

The vendors who survive this aren't the ones with the best model. They're the ones who built deep enough into workflow, close enough to the operational reality, that extracting them would cost the health system more than keeping them. That's legitimate asset specificity — earned through implementation depth and co-developed workflows, not manufactured through data control or marketing claims. (I've written about [how the ACCESS model creates a hidden tax on vendors](https://bthorson.substack.com/p/the-access-models-hidden-tax) who didn't invest in workflow integration, and about [how the Cures Act is accelerating this distinction](https://bthorson.substack.com/p/competing-on-value-not-control-how) — incumbents who invested in workflow integration are being rewarded, while those who relied on artificial switching costs are being exposed.) You can't build that kind of relationship from a banner.

### Direction 3: They build it themselves.

This is the nuclear option — vertical integration by the buyer. And it's already happening.

[Stanford Health Care built ChatEHR](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/how-to-build-a-safe-secure-medical-ai-platform), their own AI-powered EHR interface, because point solutions had become unmanageable. Nigam Shah, Stanford's chief data scientist, [put it plainly](https://www.outofpocket.health/p/open-source-in-healthcare-is-an-opportunity): the health system already runs approximately 1,500 IT systems, and every new application brings its own integration challenges, identity and access management, bug fixes, updates. "It is utter madness."

By early 2026, [1,450 clinicians were using ChatEHR](https://chatehr.stanford.edu/). Stanford didn't need the "best AI." They needed fewer vendors.

Oliver Williamson predicted this decades ago. When the transaction costs of participating in a market — the integration tax, vendor management overhead, co-innovation demands, the endless evaluation cycles — exceed the value of what the market provides, the rational firm brings the activity in-house. Stanford looked at the vendor landscape, looked at their 1,500 existing relationships, and decided the market wasn't worth it.

The most sophisticated health systems aren't going to HIMSS to find their AI partner. They're building it themselves because the market's costs exceeded the market's value. If that doesn't make you rethink your conference strategy, I'm not sure what will.

### So where does that leave us?

In a market that should be evolving toward deeper partnerships, bad marketing is pushing buyers backward in three directions simultaneously: no-decision paralysis, the incumbent's bundled offering, or bypassing the vendor market entirely.

Think about that. The vendors investing millions in conference booths are actively driving their buyers toward purchasing models where the booth is irrelevant.

The average B2B buying cycle runs [10 to 11 months](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/buyer-experience-report-2025/). Buyers aren't spending that long because they want to — they're spending it because they can't take vendor claims at face value. [69% of buyers report encountering inconsistencies](https://corporatevisions.com/blog/b2b-buying-behavior-statistics-trends/) between what a vendor says on their website and what their sales reps actually tell them. That gap between marketing and reality is what turns a three-month evaluation into a year-long one.

Every cheap signal on the conference floor accelerates this. Every "best AI" banner makes that CIO a little more comfortable with the decision to just wait for Epic to ship it.

## What Good Looks Like

This shit is hard.

I say that to prospects early. Telling someone "this is going to be hard and we're not right for everyone" might seem counterintuitive, but in a market where your aim is to build a long working relationship where both sides invest deeply — high asset specificity, if you want the economics term — and you can't take infinite bets on market winners, you're actually building credibility. You're giving yourself the opportunity to discover the right partnerships for your business instead of chasing every deal that walks by your booth.

I say it early because it *is* the qualification. Some people want to find an easy path, and if that's the case, we're not the right partners. We have the same constraints as the partnership between health systems and point solutions — we need to work with people that want to put in the effort and see the long-term value. When clients want to work through the hard problems, that's when we get into the details and build trust. That's when we talk through the rippling effects of integration choices.

Here's what that looks like in practice. We helped a client integrate with a hospital — their software identified admitted patients who would benefit from a change to their meal plan based on their vitals. Simple enough, right? The intervention sped up discharge and saved the hospital millions per year. It was actually a pretty straightforward integration.

But then the health system wanted more. Could the client change the frequency a nurse came by? Suggest a better rehab schedule? Monitor more vitals? The vendor chose to stay focused — which was the right call — but nobody saw that coming. A simple meal plan integration turned into a conversation about rethinking clinical workflows across the hospital. The success of the integration fundamentally changed the relationship. That's the ripple effect nobody markets for: what happens when your product actually *works*.

I want to go back to the ambient AI market for a second, because it's a perfect case study for everything we've been talking about. I'm not here to make enemies, so I'm going to focus on the good and leave you to evaluate the rest. [KLAS ranks these vendors](https://klasresearch.com/best-in-klas-ranking/ambient-speech/2026/487) with third-party data, the marketing approaches vary dramatically across the category, and it's the topic du jour at every conference. Quick note: Oracle Health and Epic both have ambient offerings, but those are EHR-native plays — platform features, not independent market entries. Nobody's buying Oracle's AI agent without buying their EHR. The interesting story is in the independent vendors competing for attention.

I went through the actual homepages of the top KLAS-ranked ambient AI vendors. Not press releases, not analyst summaries — the pages a buyer would land on if they Googled the company. And some patterns jumped out.

Abridge, #1 in KLAS, headlines their homepage with "Intelligence at the point of conversation." Not "best AI." Not "most advanced." They tell you where the intelligence lives — in the clinical conversation — and let the evidence do the rest. Their homepage names specific health systems, links to case studies, and leads with their KLAS Market Leader designation. Their call-to-action is "Contact us" — not "Start free trial." That's a company signaling it wants a serious conversation with serious buyers, not a volume play.

Ambience, #2, is doing something I find really sophisticated. They're running two parallel narratives — one for clinicians ("Be the best doctor you can be") and one for IT and finance leadership ("Enterprise ready from day one," "Responsible revenue starts at the point of care"). But the part that stood out to me is their informatics page. Instead of saying "we integrate with all EHRs," they describe *how* they integrate with *each* EHR. SMART on FHIR and Ambient APIs for Epic. Athenahealth Marketplace and MDP program. FHIR and REST APIs for Cerner. That level of specificity IS the costly signal — any vendor can claim integration, but you can't describe the specific API pathways per platform unless you've actually built them. And their revenue numbers? Third-party verified by AAPC and KLAS. Not self-reported.

I'm not going to pretend the correlation between marketing posture and KLAS ranking is causal. But it's visible. The vendors whose marketing leads with implementation specifics, named customers, and verifiable outcomes sit at the top. The vendors whose marketing leans on superlatives, simplification promises, and generic transformation language sit lower. I'll leave you to draw your own conclusions on the rest.

And even with all of that — the costly signals, the named health systems, the per-EHR integration detail — these vendors are still staring down Epic's decision to bundle ambient speech natively. Remember: the system of record is always lurking. The independent vendors who built deep enough into workflow might survive that. The ones who led with "best AI" already lost. The lesson isn't just "do better marketing." It's that better marketing buys you time to build the kind of relationship that marketing alone can never create.

## The Close

If you were the two physician entrepreneurs I was rambling about this to at the Elkay party, apologies. I'd had an espresso martini or two.

The overly simplistic take away of this whole blog is that hard things are hard and it's okay to say that. There's nothing wrong with a polished case study, but your buyers aren't naive (and if they are, that's not good for your long-term revenue) — they know what they are getting into. They don't want things to go sideways, but I guarantee they want to know they're working with somebody who can get into the trenches.

Vulnerability *with a plan* begets vulnerability, and you've got to have vulnerability to build a strong partnership. When you're honest about what's hard, you give the buyer permission to be honest about what they actually need — not the sanitized version they put in the RFP, but the real version where they're understaffed, their EHR is half-configured, and the last vendor left them holding a bag of broken promises. You can't get to that conversation if you're still pretending everything is easy.

And if the moral argument doesn't move you, run the numbers. When your marketing leads with superlatives and simplification, you fill the top of the funnel with buyers who were sold on easy — and then your sales team spends months qualifying them out or, worse, closing them. Now you need more SDRs to handle the volume, more AEs to run the demos, more CS resources to manage the clients who bought a vision you can't deliver. Your cost of sales goes up. Your implementation timelines blow out. The clients who bought "seamless" churn when they hit the seams, and your net revenue retention drops.

You see where this is going. Don't do it to yourself.

Do the hard thing, because your worst competitors can't. They can't describe the complexity of their implementations because they haven't done complex implementations. They can't talk about what went wrong because they don't have the depth to fix what goes wrong. That's your moat — not your AI model, not your summarization speed, not whatever metric you beat the next booth on. Your moat is that you've done the work and you can prove it. And the deals you lose by being honest about complexity? Those were never yours to begin with.
