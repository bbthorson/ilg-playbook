# VC-funded care enablement has hit its logical conclusion

*Why removing friction in multi-stakeholder markets does not create value, and what behavioral health is teaching us about it in 2026.*

---

Eight years ago I cannonballed into healthcare. My first startup, Kalamos Care, set out to make HIV-prevention medication — PrEP, and a few related modalities — easier to get. The premise felt obviously good: the medication worked, the people who needed it faced a gauntlet of appointments and stigma and paperwork, and if we stripped that friction away, more of them would get protected. We built the company around that idea. We never found much velocity, and Kalamos didn't make it.

I've spent a long time since trying to understand why, because it didn't feel like a failure of execution. It felt like a failure of premise. We built everything around one party's experience — the patient's — and assumed that making things easier for that party created value. In plenty of businesses, it does. In most of healthcare, it doesn't, and learning the difference is the most useful thing I've taken from the years since.

The instinct came from where I'd worked before. In media, the reader's attention is the entire product: remove friction, capture more attention, sell more inventory. In SaaS, swap a subscription for the ad and the logic is identical. Both are single-stakeholder worlds — one party whose experience matters, and a clean, short line from that experience to revenue. Reduce their friction and you have, almost by definition, created value. Product-led growth, growth hacking, "any friction is fatal" — all of it is downstream of that one structural fact.

Healthcare delivery has no such line. Not because it's complicated, but because there is no single party to optimize for.

Start with the word "payer," which looks like one customer and is really three or four businesses that want different things. A commercial plan administering a self-funded employer's benefit is managing that employer's cost and its ability to keep its workforce. A Medicaid managed-care organization is managing to a fixed per-member budget set by a state contract it had to win. A marketplace plan is managing a risk pool that turns over every open enrollment and is priced against a federal subsidy. Make care easier for a patient and you've helped the first, done nothing for the second, and handed the third a bigger bill. They aren't variations on a theme. They're different businesses wearing the same word.

The provider side fractures the same way. A physician paid on RVUs inside a health system, a clinician working under an MSO's management contract, and a therapist running a cash-pay practice are three businesses with three different definitions of a good day. What helps the first is irrelevant to the second and a threat to the third. There is no move you can make for "the provider," because there is no *the* provider.

So when you remove friction for the patient in this market, you aren't pulling the one lever that turns user experience into money. You're pulling one lever in a machine with a dozen of them, wired together, several pointed in opposite directions. The friction you took out for one party reappears as cost, or risk, or lost leverage, for another. That isn't a complication sitting on top of the business. It *is* the business.

## How the channel got built

To see why the behavioral health enablement platforms are breaking in 2026, you have to see what they were actually selling — and why it seemed like a good investment in 2019.

Behavioral health is a strange market to be a provider in. A primary care physician might see a given patient once or twice a year, so getting paid depends on being in-network with as many plans as possible — you have to be able to bill for whoever walks through the door. A therapist is the opposite. She sees the same small panel of patients over and over, often weekly, for months or years; a viable practice is a few dozen committed relationships, not a high-volume rotating roster. So when an independent therapist weighed the cost of going in-network — a 90-to-120-day credentialing process per insurer, each with its own paperwork, its own committee, its own contract — the math frequently didn't work. She could fill her schedule with cash-pay clients and skip the ordeal entirely. Most did. The supply of in-network therapists stayed thin, and the patients who needed in-network care couldn't find it.

Payers, meanwhile, had a problem that looked like the opposite and was really the same one: ghost networks. Their mental-health directories were full of providers who'd moved, retired, stopped taking the plan, or simply never answered. When the No Surprises Act took effect in 2022, it required directories to be accurate and updated every 90 days, and in May 2023 a Senate Finance Committee secret-shopper study made the scale impossible to ignore — staff calling 120 listed mental-health providers across a dozen plans could book an appointment 18% of the time. Under parity rules, a network that exists on paper but not in practice is a compliance exposure. Payers needed real, reachable, in-network supply. The platforms could pitch exactly that: replace your ghost network with our providers — credentialed, active, and they'll pick up the phone.

The mechanism that made it possible was delegated credentialing. Normally each payer runs its own verification on each provider — licenses, training, malpractice history — before adding them to the network, which is where the 90-to-120 days goes. Under a delegated arrangement, the payer hands that verification to a trusted entity and audits it periodically instead of doing it provider-by-provider. So Alma credentialed therapists itself, under its own group contract and NPI, and a new therapist was in-network in about 30 days instead of four months. The friction wasn't reduced so much as absorbed and amortized across thousands of providers. And it wasn't just Alma — the same entrepreneurial therapist could list on Grow, Headway, and Rula at the same time, because nothing stopped her. For an individual provider, the marginal cost of joining insurance fell toward zero.

This part is worth sitting with, because it's the whole story in miniature. The platform's product was the removal of a production cost. Its position was a channel — it stood between a fragmented supply of providers and a concentrated demand from payers, and its margin was a take-rate on the friction it had just eliminated. It's the same shape I traced in [my piece on the collapse of cold email](LINK-TO-OUROBOROS-POST): a channel whose entire value is the friction it stands on, and whose fate is therefore decided by how easily that friction can be automated away.

To a venture investor in 2019 it looked even better than that — it looked like marketplace software. High gross margins, network effects, a take-rate that compounds with volume, a market the size of American mental health, all underwritable on horizontal-SaaS multiples. But the same trait that makes a channel cheap to build makes it cheap to copy. Delegated credentialing was an operational playbook, not a moat, so the money ran in. Alma raised more than $220 million — its 2022 Series D backed by, of all people, the venture arms of Cigna and Optum. Headway raised around $325 million and was valued at $2.3 billion by 2024. Grow raised $328 million, crossed $1 billion in revenue, and hit a $3 billion valuation in early 2026. SonderMind became a unicorn back in 2021; Rula has pulled in more than $250 million. Well over a billion dollars of venture capital chasing a dozen near-identical generalist marketplaces — each investor making an individually rational bet on a proven model, all collectively funding a stampede into one channel. The same collective-action trap that now has a hundred interchangeable tools racing to fill your inbox.

And the platforms delivered — that's the part that gets lost. The providers were real. They answered the phone. The ghost-network problem was, narrowly, solved. But to solve it they pooled tens of thousands of providers under a single group NPI, and in doing so they destroyed the thing the payer needed even more than reachability: the ability to tell which provider was which. Credentialing was never only friction. It was also how a payer decided which individual providers it trusted enough to pay, and how much. The platforms removed the cost of that process and replaced it with nothing. Growth ran ahead of trust. They traded a ghost-network problem for a lemon-network problem — and when a market expands its supply while erasing the buyer's ability to tell that supply apart, a correction isn't a risk. It's a schedule. I'm arguing we're on it now.

## The four signals

In behavioral health enablement, four things happened between February and May 2026:

- **February.** Grow Therapy acquired Tenor Therapy, an AI-assisted clinical documentation startup, to embed proprietary workflow tooling into its 23,000-provider network.
- **May 1.** Spring Health closed its acquisition of Alma. The combined company has 26,000 clinicians, a JAMA-validated outcomes model, and direct lines into employer benefits budgets.
- **May 21.** Aetna announced that effective July 15, the 60-minute psychotherapy session and the 45-minute session will reimburse at the same rate for all Alma-contracted therapists. The historical 15–25% premium for extended sessions is gone.
- **May 28.** Headway began requiring all providers and all patients receiving medication management to upload government IDs and complete live facial scans to maintain access to the platform.

I wrote on LinkedIn about Spring buying Alma when it happened. The argument was that asset specificity matters, and that generalized enablement platforms had built networks without workflow differentiation. A buyer with a proprietary care model could absorb the network and convert it into specialized supply. Since I wrote that, three more data points have arrived. The pattern is not about Spring buying Alma. The pattern is about the VC-funded enablement model reaching its endpoint.

## Why now and not last year

None of this is new in concept. The structural problem has been visible for years. What's different in 2026 is that several pressures arrived at the same platforms at the same time.

Interest rates rose, and VC patience for unprofitable enablement platforms ran out. Platforms that couldn't show a path to profitability lost their cushion. Insurers spent 2020–2024 rapidly expanding behavioral health networks under MHPAEA enforcement pressure and post-pandemic demand, with limited auditing because they needed the supply. By 2025, they had the supply they needed. The audits started. Saturation hit. Jevons collapsed the per-provider economics. And ghost network risk, which payers had tolerated when supply was scarce, became actual risk in a market with oversupply.

What we're watching in 2026 is what happens when all of those pressures arrive at the same platforms at the same time. To see why the platforms are vulnerable to those pressures in the first place, you have to understand what the model was actually doing for the last six years.

What the platforms had stumbled into is Jevons' Paradox applied to administrative friction. When you reduce the production cost of something, in this case the cost of getting credentialed, you do not get less of it. You get more. A lot more.

The supply that flooded into commercial insurance networks after 2019 was not new providers. It was existing cash-pay therapists who could now economically take insurance. The networks went from constrained to vast. Headway hit 34,000 in-network providers. Grow hit 23,000. Alma hit 26,000. SonderMind and Rula and a handful of others contributed thousands more.

For a moment, this looked like a triumph. Patients had access. Providers had liquidity. Platforms had scale. Investors had a story. The friction had been removed.

And then the bill came due.

## The market for lemons, applied to therapy

George Akerlof won a Nobel Prize for explaining what happens to a market when buyers cannot tell good products from bad ones before they buy. His setup was used cars: sellers know whether their car is a peach or a lemon; buyers cannot tell the difference; rational buyers price to the average; sellers of peaches exit; the market spirals toward lemons.

The same structure governs insurance reimbursement for in-network behavioral health right now.

Aetna is the buyer. Each Alma-contracted therapist is, from Aetna's perspective, a car. When Aetna pays a claim for a 60-minute session billed under CPT code 90837, it cannot tell whether the therapist on the other end was doing trauma-focused EMDR, complex couples work, or sitting on the phone for 53 minutes because the buyer did not notice and the platform let it happen. The provider is paneled under Alma's group NPI and tax ID. From Aetna's claims data, the therapists look identical. There is no quality signal at the individual practitioner level. Aetna has no economic way to pay a peach more than a lemon, because Aetna cannot tell them apart.

So Aetna did the rational thing. Effective July 15, the 60-minute session and the 45-minute session reimburse at the same rate. For a master's-level Alma therapist in New York, Aetna's published in-network rate sits at $151.74 per session, and that rate is now flat regardless of duration. A therapist doing genuine 60-minute trauma work loses 30 to 40 dollars per session compared to what they got before. The therapist forum discussions are exactly what you would expect: disbelief, anger, and the slow recognition that there is no one to call. Alma launched an anonymous provider survey for "payer advocacy purposes," which is the corporate equivalent of saying they will be in touch.

This is what is happening underneath the data points. It is not Aetna being mean. It is Aetna doing what a rational buyer does when the seller cannot distinguish quality. It is also what Headway's payers will do next, and Grow's, and any other generalized enablement platform whose value proposition was "we made it easier for therapists to be in network."

Making it easier did not create value. It commoditized the supply, and then the buyers priced the commodity.

## The view from the therapist's office

The strategic framing misses something I think is worth naming.

A therapist who built a trauma-focused practice on the assumption that her 60-minute EMDR sessions would reimburse at the 90837 premium just had her income cut. She did not do anything differently. She did not expand her panel. She did not lower her care quality. She did the same work she had done for years. And her revenue per session dropped by 30 dollars because a payer she has no contractual relationship with decided that the platform she does have a contractual relationship with had not given them a reason to pay her any differently than the next therapist on the platform.

She did not get a vote. She does not have a number to call. The platform she contracted with cannot negotiate the rate up on her behalf because the platform's value proposition to the payer is volume and access, not quality. The platform's response, that they "disagree" with the change and are collecting de-identified provider feedback, is honest about what they can actually do. Nothing.

This is what the friction-removal playbook looks like from the provider side. When you removed the cost of credentialing, you also removed the leverage that came with each individual therapist negotiating their own rates. You could not have one without the other. The economics of being in-network became economic for the average therapist, and then the average therapist's economics became the rate.

## What the theory predicts next

If commoditized supply can't be differentiated by the payer, and the payer is responding by flattening rates, the platforms in the model have a limited menu. They can try to add substance after the fact. They can find a buyer who already has substance. They can let the payers squeeze them into reactive compliance. Or they can collapse.

What we're watching in 2026 is each of these playing out at the same time.

**Grow Therapy is trying to add substance.** In February, Grow acquired Tenor Therapy, an AI-assisted clinical documentation tool. The stated reason was to reduce provider burnout. The actual reason is to make Grow's workflow proprietary. If the documentation experience on Grow is materially better than on Alma or Headway, switching costs rise, providers stop multi-homing, and Grow has a defensible network instead of a shared one. Whether it works depends on whether Grow can build faster than competitors can copy. The bet is on.

**Alma sold.** Spring acquired Alma on May 1. Not a merger of equals, not an exit valued at a premium. Spring bought a failing marketplace at a relatively cheap multiple because Alma's best providers represent a recruiting pipeline. Think CarMax buying a failing used car dealership. Spring is keeping the dealership running independently for now, will cherry-pick the high-performing clinicians and onboard them into Spring's clinical model and enterprise contracts, and will let the rest of the marketplace operate at marketplace economics. Spring is not a peer of Alma in this transaction. Spring is the buyer of an asset Alma could no longer profitably run on its own.

**Headway is being squeezed.** In late May, Headway required every provider and every patient receiving medication management to submit a government ID and complete a live facial scan. Headway did not roll this out because they wanted to. They did it because payers, under pressure about telehealth fraud, deepfake prescribers, and pill mills, made it clear the platform's network status was at risk. Headway calls it identity verification. It is identity verification. It is also the platform being told by the buyers of its supply that the existing trust infrastructure of the network is no longer sufficient. Headway stays in business. Whether it grows from here is a different question.

**Brightline collapsed.** In April 2022, Brightline raised a Series C at a $705 million valuation as the high-flying pediatric mental health telehealth platform. Generalized care, low acuity, optimized for the freedom and ease of telehealth. By 2024 the layoffs had started: 20% of staff in 2022, more in 2023, deeper cuts in September 2024. They announced market exits, ended new telehealth patient intakes by November, pivoted toward brick-and-mortar for higher-acuity patients. They didn't pick a strategic response. The market made the choice for them, and the choice was "no longer exist in that form."

Watch for the same trajectory in any current platform that doesn't move. SonderMind, which hit a unicorn valuation in 2021 and laid off 15% of its staff in 2022, is the cautionary precedent at the marketplace layer. Rula has already started hedging, building out measurement-based care, pediatric and geriatric programs, and clinical assessments inside their EHR. The pure-play marketplace model — Alma's model — has six months to two years before the structural pressure that just hit Alma reaches everyone else.

## The access promise was hollow

The VC-funded care enablement model was built on an access promise. Patients couldn't get in-network behavioral health care, and these platforms were going to fix that by getting more therapists into networks. That was the pitch. That was why I, and people like me, thought the model was good. Removing friction for therapists meant more in-network supply meant more access for patients who needed it.

Adding supply solved the wrong half of the problem. The networks got bigger. The quality of care available through those networks went the other way.

Here's what happens at the bottom of the Akerlof spiral. When reimbursement falls, the therapists with the strongest practices leave the in-network panels first. These are the ones with full caseloads, the ones who built clinical specialties, the ones who can charge 200 or 250 dollars an hour cash. They go cash-pay. They charge what they are worth. They stop accepting insurance entirely, because the math does not work and they do not have to.

The therapists who stay in-network are, on average, the ones who could not fill a cash-pay panel at higher rates. The remaining network drifts toward lemons by selection. Quality variance within the network increases. Payer trust in the network decreases. Reimbursement falls further. The cycle continues.

The patients who lose are exactly the ones the access promise was supposed to help. The people who cannot afford to pay 200 dollars an hour out of pocket. They go to the in-network platform, get matched with a therapist, and end up, over time and on average, with progressively worse-quality care, because the best providers have exited their network. Their copay is still 30 dollars. Their access metric still looks fine to a benefit analyst. The care they receive is worse.

Frictionless access to in-network supply is not the same as access to good care. The friction-removal playbook produced the first and undermined the second. That is what it looks like when you build for one party in a multi-stakeholder market.

## What substance actually looks like

It's worth naming what the alternative looks like, because the framing so far has been about platforms in trouble. There are also platforms that never made the friction-removal bet, and they tell you what the constraints of multi-stakeholder design actually produce when you respect them up front.

Two Chairs is the cleanest example. The company employs its 600-plus clinicians as W2 employees rather than 1099 contractors. They operate hybrid telehealth and brick-and-mortar clinics across 16 states. They enforce measurement-based care across every provider, hitting 96 to 99 percent assessment adherence rates, and they publish the outcomes. 90 percent of their patients continue care through at least four sessions, against an industry average of 36 percent. 79 percent of patients experience clinically meaningful improvement by graduation. In May 2025, Two Chairs signed a partnership with Curative Insurance that offers members in-network mental health care at $0 copay and $0 deductible. That level of payer trust does not exist for any generalized marketplace platform on the market.

The economics back this up. Indeed data from early 2026 shows Two Chairs paying its W2 clinicians an average of $64.68 per hour, with active job listings reaching $76 per hour in Washington and $85 per hour in California. Clinicians are paid for the full scope of their work: clinical hours, documentation, care coordination, and no-shows. Not just delivered sessions. Once you add payroll taxes, benefits, retirement matches, and the unpaid administrative time that 1099 clinicians eat themselves, Two Chairs' fully-loaded cost per W2 clinical hour comfortably exceeds $100. A 1099 therapist receiving the standard Alma-Aetna $151.74 in New York pays self-employment tax, their own health insurance, retirement contributions, and absorbs every unpaid hour they spend on notes and admin out of that same rate. Two Chairs absorbs all of those costs for its clinicians. The math does not work for a W2 platform at standard 1099 reimbursement rates. Two Chairs has to be commanding higher tiers, whether through per-session premiums, value-based contracts, or both. The model does not function otherwise.

The structural lesson is that Two Chairs did not have to react to commoditization because they never built a commoditized business. They never went marketplace. They never optimized for frictionless provider onboarding. They built the harder version from day one. Directly employ providers. Control the clinical workflow. Prove the outcomes. Earn the payer's willingness to pay premium for predictable quality. The unit economics are different. The growth curve is slower. The company is smaller. And the payer-side dynamics that just hit Alma do not hit them at all.

Spring Health is the other example. Different shape (Spring is enterprise B2B selling directly to employers, with proprietary care models and JAMA-validated outcomes), but the same structural choice. Spring didn't pivot to substance after the market made them. They started with substance. The reason they're showing up in this piece is that they bought Alma, not because they're in the same bucket as Alma. Reading "Spring acquires Alma" as a peer merger misreads the transaction. Spring is in this story as the buyer, not the bought.

This is the choice every enablement platform faced in 2019 and did not make. The platforms that survive the next two years either find a way to substance from the marketplace side or, like Alma, get acquired by someone who already has it.

## What this means

If you are running an enablement platform right now, the question is not whether the structural pressure will reach you. It will. The question is which of the three exits you have the runway and the leadership conviction to execute. Building specificity takes time and capital you may not have. Selling to a specificity-aligned acquirer requires that one exists and that you become attractive to them before your network deteriorates. Being forced into specificity by your payers is the worst of the three, because the friction you add is reactive, not strategic, and it makes your platform feel worse for the providers you are trying to retain.

If you are an investor, stop valuing care enablement marketplaces on the same multiples you would give a horizontal SaaS business. The asset specificity is lower, the regulatory exposure is higher, and the structural endpoint of the category is consolidation into care-model players or attrition. The platforms have lower long-term unit economics than the multiples imply.

If you are a payer, the Aetna cut is a sign the current arrangement is broken, not a sign that you are winning. The reason you have to flatten rates is that the platforms cannot give you a quality signal. The Akerlof spiral that follows from there does not produce a network worth paying for. The way out is alignment. Find platforms that can prove differentiated outcomes and pay them for those outcomes. Spring Health's enterprise contracts already work this way: JAMA-validated reductions in total cost of care traded for premium reimbursement. Payer goals, platform goals, and provider goals merge around the same measurable result. The model exists. The question is whether the rest of the system can reorganize around it.

If you are a therapist, the only sustainable answer is the one you already know. Choose a platform with depth, or do not be on a platform at all. Specificity protects your rate. Generality erodes it.

## The principle that travels

Here is what I learned, slowly and at cost, since 2018.

Removing friction looks like value creation when there is only one party in the transaction. In media, the reader is the only person whose economics matter. Friction removal works there because reducing the reader's cost is, by definition, the entire value proposition.

In multi-stakeholder markets, friction removal does something else. It expands supply for one party by externalizing the cost onto the others. The supplier sees the easier path. The platform sees the scale. The supplier's customer sees the bill.

In behavioral health, the supplier was the therapist, the cost was credentialing, the platform was Alma, and the customer was the payer. The customer of the customer was the employer. The customer of the customer of the customer was the patient. Each layer up the stack, the cost of the friction-removal model got more invisible and more expensive. By the time it reached the payer, the only honest response was rate flattening. By the time it reached the patient, the only honest response was "you may not be able to find a good therapist in our network anymore."

The VC-funded care enablement category bet that frictionless supply would solve the access problem. It did not. It moved the problem upstream and made it harder to see. Now the problem is back, downstream, in the form of payer rate cuts and provider exits and a network whose quality the system cannot measure.

Doing the hard thing is the only thing that works in markets like this. By the hard thing I mean: picking a multi-stakeholder problem, accepting that no single party's friction can be reduced without affecting the others, and building a model that prices in those constraints. The platforms that survive the next two years are the ones that internalized this early. The platforms that do not will, in the kindest version, get bought.

There is a more general lesson here, and it has nothing to do with behavioral health. In any market with multiple stakeholders whose interests are not aligned, removing friction is not the same as creating value. It just looks like it for a while. That covers most B2B markets, all of healthcare, most of education, big chunks of insurance. The bill comes due when the buyer figures out they cannot tell quality from junk, and the friction you removed was the only thing keeping quality and junk apart.

Easy at scale is the wrong target. Hard for multi-stakeholder problems is the only target that survives.
