# VC-funded care enablement has hit its logical conclusion

*Why removing friction in multi-stakeholder markets does not create durable value, and what behavioral health is teaching us about it in 2026.*

---

Eight years ago I cannonballed into healthcare under the false assumption that the lesson I had learned in digital media, eliminate friction, was all that was missing in fixing US healthcare.

My first startup, Kalamos Care, set out to make HIV-prevention medication (PrEP, and a few related modalities) easier to get. The premise felt obviously good. The medication worked, the people who needed it faced a gauntlet of appointments and stigma and paperwork, and if we stripped that friction away, more of them would get protected. We never found much velocity, and Kalamos didn't make it.

It didn't feel like a failure of execution. It felt like a failure of premise. We built everything around one party's experience — the patient's — and assumed that making things easier for only that party created value.

Before starting Kalamos, I was the general manager of Moviefone. Yes, that Moviefone. We sold ad space to movie studios and tickets to moviegoers. It was a pretty simple value chain, and any friction led to less time on page, less ads, less purchases.

As a newcomer to healthcare, the economics looked pretty straightforward too. I mean, I saw ads for Roman on the subway every day. How hard could it be? See the doctor, pay the copay, get the meds, done.

> **[IMAGE: Lucille Bluth, Arrested Development: "It's one banana, Michael. What could it cost? Ten dollars?"]**

I know now that the copay is not the price. It's the last step in a purchase the patient never sees.

* An employer chose a plan.
* The plan negotiated a network.
* The network credentialed the provider.
* A claims system decided what the visit was worth.
* The provider charged the patient a copay.

Every annoying step in that chain — the 120-day credentialing queue, the referral, the prior auth — exists because someone put it there on purpose, whether it was to control cost, screen quality, or manage risk.

Simple purchases treat friction as waste. In complex purchases, friction is usually a stand-in for trust. The credentialing queue is not there because anyone likes paperwork. It's how a payer decides to trust a clinician it will never meet. So when you remove friction in healthcare, you are not cleaning up waste. You are removing a proxy for trust, and unless you replace it with something, the other party is left holding a relationship with a hole in it.

(If you work in healthcare, you already know the next part and can skip ahead. For everyone else, here is the shortest version of who is actually buying.)

"Payer" looks like one customer and is really three or four businesses. A commercial plan running a self-funded employer's benefit is managing that employer's costs. A Medicaid managed-care plan is managing to a fixed per-member budget it won in a state bid. A marketplace plan is managing a risk pool that turns over every January. Make care easier for a patient and you've helped the first, done nothing for the second, and handed the third a bigger bill. Different businesses wearing the same word. The provider side fractures the same way. A health-system physician paid on productivity credits called RVUs, a clinician under a management company's contract, and a cash-pay therapist have three different definitions of a good day. There is no move you can make for "the provider," because there is no *the* provider.

At Kalamos, none of this was theory. It was our sales problem. Our initial target customer was clinics with long appointment wait times. "Your patients like this! Let me manage your easy patients so you can spend all your time with complex ones and make similar amounts of money" was not a particularly compelling pitch. So I turned to pharmacies. "We can increase your fill rate" was compelling, right up until Caremark, the pharmacy benefit manager, reclassified the drug and forced patients into its mail-delivery program. By the time I tried to pivot to selling to the PBM itself, I was losing steam and the pandemic hit. For as much as I learned, it was one too many pivots to survive. At every stop, the friction we removed for the patient never turned into value for the person we needed to buy. The lesson underneath took me years to name. Remove access barriers without offering the other parties anything in return, and they put up new walls.

Not long after I was trying to figure out HIV care, venture capital saw an opportunity to lower the barrier to care in another area. Behavioral health. The bet was the same one I'd made. Remove friction for therapists, and access for patients follows. Six years and more than a billion dollars later, that bet just started settling. Here is how the category got built, why it is breaking, and what the survivors are doing about it.

## How the channel got built

To see why the behavioral health enablement platforms are breaking in 2026, you have to see what they were actually selling, and why it seemed like a good investment in 2019.

Behavioral health is a strange market to be a provider in.

The economics of a primary care practice:

> **2,000-patient panel × 1–2 visits a year** = revenue depends on billing whoever walks through the door. You go in-network with everyone.

The economics of a therapy practice:

> **~30 clients × one session a week** = a full schedule. Revenue depends on a few dozen committed relationships, not volume.

The in-network decision, from the therapist's chair:

> **90–120 days of credentialing per insurer, each with its own paperwork and committee** vs. **a schedule she can fill with cash-pay clients today.**

Most chose cash-pay. The supply of in-network therapists stayed thin, and the patients who needed in-network care couldn't find them.

Payers, meanwhile, had a problem that looked like the opposite and was really the same one: ghost networks. Their mental-health directories were full of providers who'd moved, retired, stopped taking the plan, or simply never answered. When the No Surprises Act took effect in 2022, it required directories to be accurate and updated every 90 days, and in May 2023 a Senate Finance Committee secret-shopper study made the scale impossible to ignore. Staff calling 120 listed mental-health providers across a dozen plans could book an appointment 18% of the time. Under parity rules (the laws requiring mental health coverage to match medical coverage), a network that exists on paper but not in practice is a compliance exposure. Payers needed real, reachable, in-network supply. The platforms could pitch exactly that. Replace your ghost network with our providers. Credentialed, active, and they'll pick up the phone.

The mechanism that made it possible was delegated credentialing. Normally each payer runs its own verification on each provider (licenses, training, malpractice history) before adding them to the network, which is where the 90-to-120 days goes. Under a delegated arrangement, the payer hands that verification to a trusted entity and audits it periodically instead of doing it provider-by-provider. So Alma credentialed therapists itself, under its own group contract and NPI (the National Provider Identifier, the ID payers use to track who they are paying), and a new therapist was in-network in about 30 days instead of four months. The friction wasn't reduced so much as absorbed and amortized across thousands of providers. And it wasn't just Alma. The same entrepreneurial therapist could list on Grow, Headway, and Rula at the same time, because at first nothing stopped her. For an individual provider, the marginal cost of joining insurance fell toward zero.

What happened next had two parts. The first is plain economics. When the cost of entering the insurance market collapsed, the supply that cost had been holding back flooded in. Headway built a network of 34,000 in-network providers. Grow built 23,000. Alma built 26,000. Tens of thousands of therapists for whom insurance had simply been uneconomical were suddenly inside commercial networks.

The second part was described in 1865. William Stanley Jevons noticed that when James Watt's improved engine doubled the work a ton of coal could do, Britain burned more coal, not less. Make a resource cheaper to use and total consumption goes up, not down. Insurance-covered therapy got dramatically cheaper to supply and to book, and the volume of reimbursed sessions exploded. Grow alone facilitated seven million visits in 2025. Payer spend on behavioral health got bigger, not more efficient.

This part is worth sitting with, because it's the whole story in miniature. The platform's product was the removal of a production cost. Its position was a channel. It stood between a fragmented supply of providers and a concentrated demand from payers, and its margin was a take-rate on the friction it had just eliminated. It's the same shape I traced in [my piece on the collapse of cold email](https://open.substack.com/pub/bthorson/p/the-ouroboros-in-your-outbox). A channel whose entire value is the friction it stands on has its fate decided by how easily that friction can be automated away.

To a venture investor in 2019 it looked even better than that. It looked like marketplace software. High gross margins, network effects, a take-rate that compounds with volume, a market the size of American mental health, all underwritable on horizontal-SaaS multiples. But the same trait that makes a channel cheap to build makes it cheap to copy. Delegated credentialing was an operational playbook, not a moat, so the money ran in. Alma raised more than $220 million, with a 2022 Series D backed by, of all people, the venture arms of Cigna and Optum. Headway raised around $325 million and was valued at $2.3 billion by 2024. Grow raised $328 million, crossed $1 billion in revenue, and hit a $3 billion valuation in early 2026. SonderMind became a unicorn back in 2021. Rula has pulled in more than $250 million. Well over a billion dollars of venture capital chased a dozen near-identical generalist marketplaces. Each investor made an individually rational bet on a proven model. Collectively, they funded a stampede into one channel.

And the platforms delivered. That's the part that gets lost. The providers were real. They answered the phone. The ghost-network problem was, narrowly, solved. The payers helped it along. They funded the platforms (Cigna and Optum put money into Alma's 2022 Series D, and HCSC, the largest customer-owned BCBS licensee, led Headway's 2023 Series C) and steered providers into them. When Dr. Ajita Robinson asked CareFirst for a rate review, she was told a review would not happen, but that contracting through Headway would bring her $17 more per session. What looked like a market preference for platforms was, in part, a market being designed to have one.

But pooling tens of thousands of providers under a single group NPI erased the thing the payer needed even more than reachability. The ability to tell which provider was which. Credentialing was the trust proxy from the top of this piece. Crude, slow, and hated, but it was how a payer decided which clinicians to pay. The platforms deleted it and replaced it with nothing. What followed was a tragedy of the commons. The commons was the payer's willingness to pay in-network rates on the assumption that the network had been filtered. Each therapist who joined made a rational choice. But each one entered the pool as one more provider the payer could not tell apart from the rest, on a master contract that priced the ten-year trauma specialist and the newly licensed generalist identically. Nobody contributed the one thing that could have justified the rates, because the signal that matters to a payer is measured outcomes, and nobody was producing it. No one made a mistake. The pool grew, the signal didn't, and growth ran ahead of trust.

For a few years, nobody minded. Insurers spent 2020 through 2024 expanding behavioral health networks under parity enforcement pressure and post-pandemic demand, and they audited lightly because they needed the supply. By 2025 they had the supply, and a buyer holding a pool of supply it cannot tell apart does what George Akerlof described in the paper that won him a Nobel Prize. His setup was used cars. Sellers know whether their car is a peach or a lemon. Buyers can't tell the difference, so they price to the average. Sellers of peaches exit. The market spirals toward lemons.

> **[IMAGE: "slaps roof of car" meme. Car salesman: "this network can fit so much undifferentiated supply in it"]**

From Aetna's claims data, every therapist under Alma's group NPI looks identical. Aetna cannot tell whether a 60-minute session billed under CPT code 90837 was trauma-focused EMDR or 53 minutes of comfortable silence. It has no economic way to pay a peach more than a lemon. The theory says a rational buyer in that position flattens its prices to the average and lets the peaches walk. The platforms had traded a ghost-network problem for a lemon-network problem, and from there a correction wasn't a risk. It was a schedule. I'm arguing we're on it now.

## Three signals in four months

In behavioral health enablement, three things happened between February and May 2026.

**Grow Therapy acquires Tenor Therapy**
*February 2026*
An AI-assisted clinical documentation startup, bought to embed proprietary workflow tooling into Grow's 23,000-provider network.

**Spring Health closes its acquisition of Alma**
*May 1, 2026*
The combined company has 26,000 clinicians, a JAMA-validated outcomes model, and direct lines into employer benefits budgets.

**Aetna flattens the psychotherapy rate**
*May 21, 2026*
Effective July 15, the 60-minute session and the 45-minute session reimburse at the same rate for all Alma-contracted therapists. The historical 15–25% premium for extended sessions is gone.

(A fourth, quieter signal matters too. Headway began requiring government IDs and live facial scans from its providers in late May. I'll come back to it, but it's a supporting character.)

I wrote on LinkedIn about Spring buying Alma when it happened. The argument was that asset specificity matters. In plain English, a network tailored to a specific care model is worth more to the buyer who owns that model than to anyone else, and a generalized network tailored to nothing is worth a discount. The enablement platforms had built networks without workflow differentiation. A buyer with a proprietary care model could absorb the network and convert it into specialized supply. Since I wrote that, more data points have arrived. The pattern is not about Spring buying Alma. The pattern is about the VC-funded enablement model reaching its endpoint.

Look at the Aetna move closely, because it is the lemons mechanism running in public. For a master's-level Alma therapist in New York, Aetna's published in-network rate sits at $151.74 per session, and as of July 15 that rate is flat regardless of duration. A therapist doing genuine 60-minute trauma work loses 30 to 40 dollars per session. The forum threads are exactly what you would expect. Disbelief, anger, and the slow recognition that there is no one to call. Alma launched an anonymous provider survey for "payer advocacy purposes," which is the corporate equivalent of saying they will be in touch.

The standard read on this move is upcoding enforcement. Payers have spent a decade watching 45-minute sessions get billed as 60, and flattening the rate is a familiar cost-containment lever. That read is correct, and it is the lemon problem wearing claims-ops clothing. Upcoding is what a buyer suspects when it cannot verify what it bought. If Aetna could tell the honest trauma therapist from the upcoder, it would price them differently. It cannot, so it priced the whole pool to the average. Notice the scope. The cut applies to Alma-contracted therapists, not to Aetna's entire book. A pure code-level utilization lever would be plan-wide. This one is aimed at the pool whose supply Aetna cannot tell apart.

Asked about the change, Aetna said its rate policies are "based on the level of care provided with an emphasis on shared and measured outcomes aided by technology, not how providers connect with members." That sentence is the whole piece in payer-speak. Produce measured outcomes, or be priced as the average.

There is a cruel symmetry here. When the platforms removed the cost of credentialing, they also removed the leverage that came with each therapist negotiating her own rates. You could not have one without the other. Being in-network became economical for the average therapist, and then the average therapist's economics became the rate. It is not Aetna being mean. It is what a rational buyer does when the seller cannot distinguish quality. Headway's payers will do it next, and Grow's, and every other platform whose pitch was "we made it easier." Making it easier did not create durable value. It commoditized the supply, and the buyers priced the commodity.

## The scramble toward trust

If removing friction created durable value, the story would end here. The networks exist. The supply is liquid. Instead, watch what every company in the category is doing in 2026. Each one is trying to acquire the thing the deleted friction used to carry. They are all, at different speeds and price points, buying trust.

### Grow Therapy: buying lock-in and an outcomes story

In February, Grow acquired Tenor Therapy, an AI documentation tool. The stated reason was provider burnout. Better documentation raises switching costs in a multi-homed provider pool (UnitedHealthcare is pushing the same direction, now allowing a solo provider to credential through only one platform), and Grow has started publishing outcome claims, with 80% of clients showing measurable symptom improvement within 30 days. A month after the Tenor deal, TCV and Goldman Sachs led a $150 million round at a $3 billion valuation, and the announcement barely mentions the marketplace. The market is pricing Grow's escape from it.

### Spring Health: already had the trust signal, so it bought the supply

Spring built measurement from day one. Proprietary care models, outcomes validated in JAMA, enterprise contracts that trade proven cost reductions for premium rates. Alma had 26,000 clinicians and a payer flattening its rates. Deal terms are undisclosed, so read this as my interpretation. Think CarMax buying a used car dealership for its inventory. Spring keeps the dealership running, moves the best clinicians into its clinical model and enterprise contracts, and lets the rest run at marketplace economics. Spring is in this story as the buyer, not the bought.

### Rula: building measurement before payers demand it

Standardized assessments inside its EHR, plus expansions into pediatric and geriatric programs. The platforms can see where the value moved.

### Headway: no outcomes to sell, so it is being made to prove identity instead

This is the quieter fourth signal from earlier. In late May, Headway required every provider, and every patient receiving medication management, to submit a government ID and complete a live facial scan. To be fair, this is not purely a commoditization story. The DEA's forthcoming tele-prescribing rules are expected to require identity verification, and Headway is getting ahead of a rule it cannot avoid. But the payer half of the pressure is the tell. When the buyers of your supply demand biometric proof that your providers are who they say they are, the network's trust infrastructure has failed at the most basic level there is. Identity is the floor below quality. Grow is buying outcome measurement. Headway is being made to prove its therapists exist.

### Brightline: stood still, and the market made its choice for it

In April 2022, Brightline was a $705 million pediatric telehealth platform. Generalized care, low acuity, optimized for ease. Then three consecutive years of layoffs, market exits, the end of new telehealth intakes by late 2024, and a forced pivot to brick-and-mortar for higher-acuity patients. The choice was "no longer exist in that form."

### Two Chairs: never removed the friction, and payers pay a premium for it

The company employs its 600-plus clinicians as W2 employees, runs physical clinics in three states with virtual care in more than twenty, enforces measurement-based care (outcome measures are collected in 95 percent of sessions), and publishes the results. Ninety percent of patients continue through at least four sessions against an industry average of 36 percent, and roughly three in four show meaningful improvement by the end of treatment. In May 2025, Curative Insurance began offering Two Chairs to members at $0 copay and $0 deductible. That trust does not exist for any marketplace platform. The economics only close if payers pay premium rates, because the fully loaded cost of a W2 clinical hour (payroll taxes, benefits, supervision, and paid admin time on top of wages) exceeds what standard reimbursement supports. Evidently payers pay it. Slower growth, harder model, no Aetna problem.

The pattern is the same in every case. Removing friction produced a temporary market change. A supply flood, a few years of growth, a category of unicorns. It did not produce a durable one, because durable position in a multi-stakeholder market is trust, and trust here has a specific currency: measured, published outcomes. The companies that had the currency from the start are the buyers. The companies racing to mint it might make it. The companies that can't are getting repriced, acquired, or wound down.

## The access promise was hollow

Care enablement looked like a scale game. Pump venture money into removing therapist friction, own the supply, and patient access follows. That was the pitch, and it's why I, and people like me, thought the model was good.

But remember Jevons. When the resource gets cheaper, consumption goes up, and the externalities land on whoever holds the budget. Britain got cheaper steam engines and a smokier sky. Payers got bigger networks and more billed sessions, with no evidence that anyone was getting better. So rates fall. And when rates fall, the therapists who can fill a cash-pay panel at 200 dollars an hour leave first, the network drifts toward lemons by selection, payer trust falls further, and rates follow it down. Providers can see the spiral from inside. As practice owner John Puls put it to Behavioral Health Business in May, "If it's at a point where they really have no choice but to be on Headway and Alma, why would they continue to pay these higher rates?"

Frictionless access to in-network supply is not the same as access to good care. The playbook produced the first and undermined the second. Supply was never the bottleneck. Trust was.

## The principle that travels

Here is what I learned, slowly and at cost, since 2018.

At Moviefone, removing friction created value, because the person feeling the friction was the person paying. One party, one experience, one short line to revenue.

In multi-stakeholder markets, the friction is load-bearing. It is usually a crude, slow, infuriating stand-in for trust between parties who have not found a way to truly establish it. People who come from tech, and I was one of them, believe the answer is to remove the barriers. That instinct makes the problem worse. Remove the proxy without building real trust and you have not created value. You have created a hole where trust used to be, and the bill for that hole goes to whoever was standing furthest from your product. In behavioral health the bill moved upstream, therapist to platform to payer, until Aetna repriced it. Now it is moving back downstream as rate cuts, provider exits, and networks whose quality nobody can measure.

This is true for any business that makes a complex process easier without increasing trust, not just care enablement. And the counterparty will not sit still. When the payer plugs the hole with flat rates and facial scans, that is not the system stabilizing. That is the other side playing your game back at you, and every round of the cat-and-mouse destroys more of the trust the market runs on. You do not win that game by reacting faster. You end it by producing the thing trust can actually be built from. In this market, that is measured, published outcomes.

The tell was in the category's name the whole time. "Enablement" means friction reducing, and in a multi-stakeholder market, friction reducing is trust eroding. Now look at the word the rest of healthcare technology is organized around: interoperability. On paper it is about data sharing, standards and APIs and pipes. But sharing data is not required of anyone. It is desired, and when a hospital or a payer or a vendor declines to share or to integrate, the reason is, at least in part, that they do not trust what happens next. The pipes are rarely the constraint. The willingness is. And the unwillingness is rarely philosophical. A health system that hoards data is defending its hold on the patient panel, using data friction as a wall around its market share. That is still a trust problem. In markets, trust is not faith in good intentions. It is confidence that the exchange will not be used against you, and that confidence has to be built, demonstrated, and paid for. That test travels well beyond behavioral health. Your interoperability strategy must be trust building, not trust eroding. If you make a complex process easier, the question is not how much friction you removed. It is what you gave the other parties to trust instead.

Eight years ago I built a company on the assumption that making things easier for one party created value. It does not. It moves value around, and the bill lands on whoever you were not looking at. Easy at scale is the wrong target. Trust at scale is the only target that survives.
