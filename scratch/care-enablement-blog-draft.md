# VC-funded care enablement has hit its logical conclusion

*Why removing friction in multi-stakeholder markets does not create durable value, and what behavioral health is teaching us about it in 2026.*

---

Eight years ago I cannonballed into healthcare under the false assumption that US healthcare was missing the one lesson I had learned in digital media: eliminate friction.

My first startup, Kalamos Care, set out to make HIV-prevention medication (PrEP, and a few related modalities) easier to get. The premise felt obviously good. The medication worked, the people who needed it faced a gauntlet of appointments and stigma and paperwork, and if we stripped that friction away, more of them would get protected. We never found much velocity, and Kalamos didn't make it.

The failure ran deeper than execution. The premise itself was broken. We built everything around one party's experience — the patient's — and assumed that making things easier for only that party created value.

Before starting Kalamos, I was the general manager of Moviefone. Yes, that Moviefone. We sold ad space to movie studios and tickets to moviegoers. It was a pretty simple value chain, and any friction led to less time on page, fewer ads, fewer purchases.

As a newcomer to healthcare, the economics looked pretty straightforward too. I mean, I saw ads for Roman on the subway every day. How hard could it be? See the doctor, pay the copay, get the meds, done.

> **[IMAGE: Lucille Bluth, Arrested Development: "It's one banana, Michael. What could it cost? Ten dollars?"]**

I know now that the copay is not the price. It's the last step in a purchase the patient never sees.

* An employer chose a plan.
* The plan negotiated a network.
* The network credentialed the provider.
* A claims system decided what the visit was worth.
* The provider charged the patient a copay.

Every annoying step in that chain, from the credentialing queue to the referral to the prior auth, exists because someone put it there on purpose, whether it was to control cost, screen quality, or manage risk.

Simple purchases treat friction as waste. In complex purchases, friction is usually a stand-in for trust. Take that credentialing queue. Before a payer adds a clinician to its network, it verifies her licenses, training, and malpractice history, then routes the file through a committee. That takes 90 to 120 days, per insurer, each with its own paperwork. Nobody designed that out of love for paperwork. It's how a payer decides to trust a clinician it will never meet. So when you remove friction in healthcare, you are pulling out a proxy for trust, and unless you replace it with something, the other party is left holding a relationship with a hole in it.

(If you work in healthcare, you already know the next part and can skip ahead. For everyone else, here is the shortest version of who is actually buying.)

"Payer" looks like one customer and is really many different businesses.

* A commercial plan running a self-funded employer's benefit is managing that employer's costs.
* A Medicaid managed-care plan is managing to a fixed per-member budget it won in a state bid.
* A marketplace plan is managing a risk pool that turns over every January.

Make care easier for a patient and you've helped the first, done nothing for the second, and handed the third a bigger bill.

Different businesses, referred to with the same word. The provider side fractures the same way. A health-system physician paid on productivity credits called RVUs, a clinician under a management company's contract, and a cash-pay therapist have three different definitions of a good day. There is no move you can make for "the provider," because there is no *the* provider.

At Kalamos, none of this was theory. It was our sales problem. Our initial target was clinics with long appointment wait times. "Your patients like this! Let me manage your easy patients so you can spend all your time with complex ones and make similar amounts of money" was not a particularly compelling pitch. So I turned to pharmacies. "We can increase your fill rate" was compelling, right up until Caremark, the pharmacy benefit manager, reclassified the drug and forced patients into its mail-delivery program.

By the time I tried to pivot to selling to the PBM itself (good luck, old me), I was losing steam and the pandemic hit. For as much as I learned, it was one too many pivots to survive. At every stop, the friction we removed for the patient never turned into sustainable value for the real buyer we needed. The lesson underneath took me years to name. Remove access barriers without offering the other parties anything in return, and they put up new walls.

Around the time I was trying to figure out HIV care, venture capital saw an opportunity to lower the barrier to care in another area. Behavioral health. The bet was the same one I'd made. Remove friction for therapists, and access for patients follows. Six years and more than a billion dollars later, that bet just started settling. Here is how the category got built, why it is breaking, and what the survivors are doing about it.

## How the channel got built

To see why the behavioral health enablement platforms are breaking in 2026, you have to see what they were actually selling, and why it seemed like a good investment in 2019.

Start with the shortage. Demand for therapy has outrun the supply of therapists for years, and the gap was worst exactly where most patients look, inside their insurance network. The mismatch wasn't an accident of workforce planning. It came from the economics of being a behavioral health provider.

The economics of a primary care practice:

> **2,000-patient panel × 1–2 visits a year** = revenue depends on billing whoever walks through the door. You go in-network with everyone.

The economics of a therapy practice:

> **~30 clients × one session a week** = a full schedule. Revenue depends on a few dozen committed relationships, not volume.

The in-network decision, from the therapist's chair:

> **That 90-to-120-day credentialing gauntlet, repeated for every insurer** vs. **a schedule she can fill with cash-pay clients today.**

Most chose cash-pay. The supply of in-network therapists stayed thin, and the patients who needed in-network care couldn't find them.

Payers, meanwhile, had a problem that looked like the opposite and was really the same one: ghost networks. Their mental-health directories were full of providers who'd moved, retired, stopped taking the plan, or simply never answered. When the No Surprises Act took effect in 2022, it required directories to be accurate and updated every 90 days, and in May 2023 a Senate Finance Committee secret-shopper study **[LINK]** made the scale impossible to ignore. Staff calling 120 listed mental-health providers across a dozen plans could book an appointment 18% of the time. Under parity rules (the laws requiring mental health coverage to match medical coverage), a network that exists on paper but not in practice is a compliance exposure. Payers needed real, reachable, in-network supply.

What happened next was a perfect storm. Payers needed supply badly enough to drop their guard, and technology arrived holding the key to the locked door. Delegated credentialing. Normally each payer runs its own verification on each provider before adding them to the network, which is where the 90 to 120 days goes. Under a delegated arrangement, the payer hands that verification to a trusted entity and audits it periodically instead of doing it provider-by-provider. So Alma credentialed therapists itself, under its own group contract and NPI (the National Provider Identifier, the ID payers use to track who they are paying), and a new therapist was in-network in about 30 days instead of four months. The friction wasn't reduced so much as absorbed and amortized across thousands of providers. And it wasn't just Alma. The same entrepreneurial therapist could list on Grow, Headway, and Rula at the same time, because at first nothing stopped her. For an individual provider, the marginal cost of joining insurance fell toward zero.

Now the platforms could pitch payers exactly what they needed. Replace your ghost network with our providers. Credentialed, active, and they'll pick up the phone.

What happened next had two parts. The first is plain economics. When the cost of entering the insurance market collapsed, the supply that cost had been holding back flooded in. Headway built a network of 34,000 in-network providers. Grow built 23,000. Alma built 26,000. Tens of thousands of therapists for whom insurance had simply been uneconomical were suddenly inside commercial networks.

The second part was described in 1865. William Stanley Jevons noticed that when James Watt's improved steam engine doubled the work a ton of coal could do, Britain burned more coal, not less. Make a resource cheaper to use and total consumption goes up, not down. Insurance-covered therapy got dramatically cheaper to supply and to book, and the volume of reimbursed sessions exploded. Grow alone facilitated seven million visits in 2025. Payer spend on behavioral health got bigger, not more efficient.

This part is worth sitting with. The platform's product was the removal of a credentialing production cost. Rather than creating an asset, these networks acted as channels. They stood between a fragmented supply of providers and patients who wanted to use the coverage they were already paying for, with payers holding the budget in the middle, and their margin was a take-rate on the friction they had just eliminated. It's the same shape I traced in [my piece on the collapse of cold email](https://open.substack.com/pub/bthorson/p/the-ouroboros-in-your-outbox). A channel whose entire value is the friction it stands on has its fate decided by how easily that friction can be automated away.

To a venture investor in 2019, I'm sure it looked even better than that. It looked like a network SaaS play. High gross margins, network effects, a take-rate that compounds with volume, a market the size of American mental health, all underwritable on horizontal-SaaS multiples. And the funding model made the underlying problem worse. Venture math needs quick, huge wins, and trust is the one asset that cannot be built quickly. Every dollar spent proving outcomes to a payer is a dollar not spent flooding the channel, so the capital optimized for the flood. Meanwhile, the same trait that makes a channel cheap to build makes it cheap to copy. Delegated credentialing was an operational playbook, not a moat, so the money ran in. Alma raised more than $220 million, with a 2022 Series D backed by, of all people, the venture arms of Cigna and Optum. Headway raised around $325 million and was valued at $2.3 billion by 2024. Grow raised $328 million, crossed $1 billion in revenue, and hit a $3 billion valuation in early 2026. SonderMind, the earliest entrant, hit unicorn status back in 2021 and previewed the ending for everyone else, cutting 15% of its workforce in late 2022 as payer leverage hardened and growth stalled. Rula has pulled in more than $250 million. Well over a billion dollars of venture capital chased a dozen near-identical generalist marketplaces. Each investor made an individually rational bet on a proven model. Collectively, they funded a stampede into one channel.

And the platforms delivered, with the payers' help. The providers were real, they answered the phone, and the ghost-network problem was, narrowly, solved. Payers funded the platforms (beyond the Cigna and Optum money in Alma, HCSC, the largest customer-owned BCBS licensee, led Headway's 2023 Series C) and steered providers into them. When [Dr. Ajita Robinson asked CareFirst for a rate review](https://bhbusiness.com/2026/05/27/small-providers-flock-to-therapist-enablement-platforms-at-the-cost-of-autonomy/), she was told a review would not happen, but that contracting through Headway would bring her $17 more per session. What looked like a market preference for platforms was, in part, a market being designed to have one.

But pooling tens of thousands of providers under a single group NPI erased something the payer needed even more than reachability. Evidence that the new spend was buying anything. A payer that watches its behavioral health line grow needs to see the offset somewhere else in the book, fewer ER visits, lower total cost of care, and it needs to know which providers are producing it. Under a group NPI it can see neither. Credentialing was the trust proxy from the top of this piece. Crude, slow, and hated, but it was how a payer decided which clinicians to pay. The platforms gamed it and replaced it with nothing.

What followed was a tragedy of the commons. The commons was the payer's willingness to pay in-network rates on the assumption that the network had been filtered. Each therapist who joined made a rational choice. But each one entered the pool as one more provider the payer could not tell apart from the rest, on a master contract that priced the ten-year trauma specialist and the newly licensed generalist identically. Measured outcomes could have justified those rates, and the contract never asked for them. The specialist had no way to prove she was worth more, the generalist had no reason to prove anything, and the platform had no incentive to make either of them try. No individual made a mistake. The pool grew, the signal didn't, and growth ran ahead of trust.

Insurers spent 2020 through 2024 expanding behavioral health networks under parity enforcement pressure and post-pandemic demand, and they audited lightly because they needed the supply. By 2025 they had the supply, and a buyer holding a pool of supply it cannot tell apart does what George Akerlof described in the paper that won him a Nobel Prize. His setup was used cars. Sellers know whether their car is a peach or a lemon. Buyers can't tell the difference, so they price to the average. Sellers of peaches exit to seek premium prices elsewhere. The market spirals toward lemons.

Not everyone was selling into that spiral. While the marketplaces flooded the channel, Spring Health was running the opposite play. A proprietary care model, outcomes validated in JAMA, and contracts that sold employers proven cost reduction instead of selling payers raw supply. Hold that thought, because Spring is how this story ends.

> **[IMAGE: "slaps roof of car" meme. Car salesman: "this network can fit so much undifferentiated supply in it"]**

Extrapolate back to behavioral health. In Aetna's claims data, every therapist under Alma's group NPI looks identical. Aetna cannot tell whether a 60-minute session billed under CPT code 90837 was trauma-focused EMDR or 53 minutes of comfortable silence. It has no economic way to pay a peach more than a lemon. The theory says a rational buyer in that position flattens its prices to the average and lets the peaches walk. The platforms had traded a ghost-network problem for a lemon-network problem, and from there a correction wasn't a risk. It was on a predictable schedule.

## Three signals in four months

In behavioral health enablement, three things happened between February and May 2026.

**Grow Therapy acquires Tenor Therapy**
*February 2026*
An AI-assisted clinical documentation startup, bought to embed proprietary workflow tooling into Grow's 23,000-provider network.

**Spring Health closes its acquisition of Alma**
*May 1, 2026*
The measurement company from a few paragraphs ago buys a 26,000-clinician network, pairing its JAMA-validated outcomes model and employer distribution with the largest pool of supply on the market.

**Aetna flattens the psychotherapy rate**
*May 21, 2026*
Effective July 15, the 60-minute session and the 45-minute session reimburse at the same rate for all Alma-contracted therapists. The historical 15–25% premium for extended sessions is gone.

I wrote on LinkedIn **[LINK]** about Spring buying Alma when it happened. The argument was that asset specificity matters, particularly when a market can't produce reliable trust signals. In plain English, a network tailored to a specific care model is worth more to the buyer who owns that model than to anyone else, and a generalized network tailored to nothing is worth a discount. The enablement platforms had built networks without workflow differentiation. A buyer with a proprietary care model could absorb the network and convert it into specialized supply. Since I wrote that, more data points have arrived. The pattern is bigger than Spring buying Alma. It is the VC-funded enablement model reaching its endpoint.

Look at the Aetna move closely, because it is the lemons mechanism running in public. Among commercial payers, [Aetna's psychotherapy reimbursement runs highest](https://bhbusiness.com/2026/06/09/aetna-reimbursement-highest-for-psychotherapy-sessions-among-commercial-payers/), and Alma therapists had negotiated some of the highest platform rates in the market, which means Aetna had the most to claw back. For a master's-level Alma therapist in New York, the published in-network rate sits at $151.74 per session, and as of July 15 that rate is flat regardless of duration. The flattening runs along a second axis too. Doctoral-level providers will now reimburse at the same rate as master's-level ones. Duration, credential, both priced to the average. A therapist doing genuine 60-minute trauma work loses $30 to $40 per session. The forum threads are exactly what you would expect. Disbelief, anger, and the slow recognition that there is no one to call. The professional associations asked Aetna to reconsider. Alma launched an anonymous provider survey for "payer advocacy purposes." Both are the corporate equivalent of saying they will be in touch.

The standard read on this move is upcoding enforcement. Payers have spent a decade watching 45-minute sessions get billed as 60, and flattening the rate is a familiar cost-containment lever. You don't have to squint too hard to see it is the lemon problem wearing claims-ops clothing. Upcoding is what a buyer suspects when it cannot verify what it bought. If Aetna could tell the honest trauma therapist from the upcoder, it would price them differently. It cannot, so it priced the whole pool to the average.

Notice the scope. The cut applies to Alma-contracted therapists, not to Aetna's entire book. A pure code-level utilization lever would be plan-wide. This one is aimed at the pool whose supply Aetna cannot tell apart.

Asked about the change, [Aetna said](https://bhbusiness.com/2026/05/27/small-providers-flock-to-therapist-enablement-platforms-at-the-cost-of-autonomy/) its rate policies are "based on the level of care provided with an emphasis on shared and measured outcomes aided by technology, not how providers connect with members." That sentence is the whole piece in payer-speak. Produce measured outcomes, or be priced as the average.

There is a cruel symmetry here. When the platforms removed the cost of credentialing, they also removed the leverage that came with each therapist negotiating her own rates. You could not have one without the other. Being in-network became economical for the average therapist, and then the average therapist's economics became the rate. It is not Aetna being mean. It is what a rational buyer does when the seller cannot distinguish quality. Headway's payers will do it next, and Grow's, and every other platform whose pitch was "we made it easier." Making it easier did not create durable value. It commoditized the supply, and the buyers priced the commodity.

## The scramble toward trust

If removing friction created durable value, the story would end here. It doesn't, because of what makes any market work. A competitive market runs on information symmetry. Both sides can see what is being traded, so both sides can price it. This market has liquidity without information. The supply is abundant, bookable, and indistinguishable, which means the only stable price is the average and the only direction is down. So watch what every company in the category is doing in 2026. Each one is trying to acquire the thing the deleted friction used to carry. They are all, at different speeds and price points, buying trust.

### Grow Therapy: buying lock-in and an outcomes story

In February, Grow acquired Tenor Therapy, an AI documentation tool. The stated reason was provider burnout. To me, the strategic reason looks like increasing asset specificity. Today the available provider pool is multi-homed. The same therapist lists on Grow, Headway, and Rula at once. Better documentation tooling might incentivize her to stay. It also gives Grow a lever to measure performance and police its network itself.

The payers are forcing the same choice from the other side. Since late summer 2025, UnitedHealthcare has [limited independent providers to one platform's group contract at a time](https://www.reddit.com/r/PMHNP/comments/1qv073p/headwayalma_and_uhc/), a direct consequence of the group-NPI pooling from earlier: credential through "Group Headway" and UHC's systems treat your NPI as exclusive to that contract. Grow has also started publishing outcome claims, with 80% of clients showing measurable symptom improvement within 30 days. A month after the Tenor deal, TCV and Goldman Sachs led a $150 million round at a $3 billion valuation, and the announcement barely mentions the marketplace. The market is pricing Grow's escape from it. Rula is making the same move more quietly, building standardized assessments into its electronic health record before payers demand them. The platforms can see where the value moved.

### Spring Health: already had the trust signal, so it bought the supply

Spring spent a decade building what the marketplaces skipped. Founded in 2016, it sold employers a measurement-based care model, published its outcomes in JAMA, and signed enterprise contracts that traded proven cost reduction for premium rates. By 2026 it held the one asset the category was starving for, a trust signal payers and employers accept, and no cheap way to add supply.

Alma had the inverse, 26,000 clinicians and a payer flattening its rates. Deal terms are undisclosed, so read this as my interpretation. Think CarMax buying a used car dealership. CarMax's edge was never the cars. It was the certification. Buyers trust the inspection process enough to pay a premium, which lets CarMax buy undifferentiated inventory cheap, certify the good units, and capture the spread. Spring keeps the dealership running, moves the clinicians who fit its clinical model into its enterprise contracts, and lets the rest run at marketplace economics. Spring is in this story as the buyer, not the bought, and the difference was never network size. It was who could certify the inventory.

### Headway: no outcomes to sell, so it is being made to prove identity instead

There was a fourth signal this spring, quieter than the three above. In late May, Headway began requiring every provider, and every patient receiving medication management, to submit a government ID and complete a live facial scan. To be fair, this is not purely a commoditization story. The DEA's forthcoming tele-prescribing rules are expected to require identity verification, and Headway is getting ahead of a rule it cannot avoid. But the payer half of the pressure is the tell. When the buyers of your supply demand biometric proof that your providers are who they say they are, the network's trust infrastructure has failed at the most basic level there is. Identity is the floor below quality.

Headway can read the board. At an industry conference this spring, its own head of clinical strategy described directory accuracy and 48-hour appointments as "not really meaningful" in the big picture, and said the field is "moving from measuring activities to measuring impact." When the company whose entire product was access metrics starts talking that way, the access era is over. For now, Grow is buying outcome measurement. Headway is being made to prove its therapists exist.

### Policing your network, or training great employees

There have always been two ways to make a network trustworthy. Police it from the outside, which is what Grow's measurement tooling and Headway's facial scans attempt after the fact. Or employ it, train it, and stand behind it, which is what Two Chairs has done from the start.

Two Chairs is close to a control group for this story. Founded in 2017, the same vintage as the marketplaces, it raised about $92 million over nine years while the platforms raised that in single rounds, because it chose every expensive option available. It employs its 600-plus clinicians as W2 employees, runs physical clinics in three states with virtual care in more than twenty, and enforces measurement-based care, collecting outcome measures in 95% of sessions. The cost of those choices is real. The fully loaded cost of a W2 clinical hour (payroll taxes, benefits, supervision, and paid admin time on top of wages) exceeds what standard reimbursement supports, which is why the model stayed small while the marketplaces minted unicorns, and why it only works if payers pay premium rates.

They do, because of what the spending buys. 90% of Two Chairs patients continue through at least four sessions against an industry average of 36%, and roughly three in four show meaningful improvement by the end of treatment. In May 2025, Curative Insurance began offering Two Chairs to members at $0 copay and $0 deductible. That trust does not exist for any marketplace platform. Spring runs the same logic at enterprise scale. Own the care model, measure everything, sell the results. Slower growth, harder model, no Aetna problem.

The pattern is the same in every case. Removing friction produced a temporary market change. A supply flood, a few years of growth, a category of unicorns. It did not produce a durable one, because durable position in a multi-stakeholder market is trust, and trust here has a specific currency: measured, published outcomes. Even that is the entry ticket. The arrangements payers trust most are full-risk contracts, where a provider takes responsibility for a patient's total cost of care and loses money if the outcomes don't materialize. Risk is the one trust signal that cannot be faked, because faking it costs the faker. The companies that had the currency from the start are the buyers. The companies racing to mint it might make it. The companies that can't are getting repriced, acquired, or wound down.

## The access promise was hollow

Care enablement looked like a scale game. Pump venture money into removing therapist credentialing friction, own the supply, and patient access follows. That was the pitch, and in 2019, I thought the model was brilliant.

But remember Jevons. In a world of unconstrained demand, when the resource gets cheaper, consumption goes up, and someone absorbs the externalities. Britain got cheaper steam engines and a smokier sky. Payers got bigger networks and more billed sessions, with no evidence that anyone was getting better. The difference is that the smoke fell on a public that could do nothing about it. These externalities fell on payers, who can do plenty. They cut rates, tighten medical-necessity review, and find new ways to restrict credentialing. And when rates fall, the therapists who can fill a cash-pay panel at $200 an hour leave first, the network drifts toward lemons by selection, payer trust falls further, and rates follow it down.

Frictionless access to in-network supply is not the same as access to good care. The playbook produced the first and undermined the second. Supply was constrained because building trust was hard. Increase the supply without building the trust and there is only one way it ends.

## The principle that travels

Here is what I learned, slowly and at cost, since 2018.

At Moviefone, removing friction created value, because the person feeling the friction was the person paying. One party, one experience, one short line to revenue.

In multi-stakeholder markets, the friction is load-bearing. It is usually a crude, slow, infuriating stand-in for trust between parties who have not found a way to truly establish it. People who come from tech, like me, believe the answer is to remove as much friction as possible. That instinct makes the problem worse. Remove the proxy without building real trust and all you have created is a hole where trust used to be, and the bill for the hole goes to whichever party was leaning on the proxy you removed. In behavioral health that was the payer, and the bill moved upstream, therapist to platform to payer, until Aetna repriced it. Now it is moving back downstream as rate cuts, provider exits, and networks whose quality nobody can measure.

This is true for any business that makes a complex process easier without increasing trust, not just care enablement. And the counterparty will not sit still. When the payer plugs the hole with flat rates and facial scans, the other side has started playing your game back at you, and every round of the cat-and-mouse destroys more of the trust the market runs on. You do not win that game by reacting faster. You end it by producing the thing trust can actually be built from. In behavioral health, the signal is unambiguous. Measured, published outcomes.

The tell was in the category's name all along. "Enablement" promised friction removal, and friction removal without trust is erosion. The same test is coming for the word the rest of healthcare technology organizes itself around, interoperability, because the pipes are rarely the constraint. The willingness to share is, and willingness is a trust problem.

Eight years ago I built a company on the assumption that making things easier for one party created value. It does not. It moves value around, and the bill lands on whoever you were not looking at. So before you remove a step from someone else's process, ask whose trust that step was carrying, and what you are handing them in its place. If the answer is nothing, you have already scheduled the correction.

Easy at scale is the wrong target. Trust at scale is the only target that survives.
