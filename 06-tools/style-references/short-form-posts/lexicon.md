Standards, networks, and the coordination problem

Healthcare interoperability has followed the same pattern for decades: convene the smartest people in a room, spend years crafting the perfect specification, mandate adoption, hope for convergence. The bet is always that if we can just get everyone to agree on the specification, interoperability will follow.

AT Protocol takes a different approach. Instead of pre-defining every possible data type, it standardizes how you publish a schema.

Anyone can create a "Lexicon" - a machine-readable specification. Apps discover these Lexicons, evaluate them, and choose which to support. The best ones win through adoption, not committee decree.

CMS Aligned Networks have a brilliant goal: seamless data flow across a "network of networks." The framework establishes trust, legal reciprocity, security standards - all the liability and safety protections healthcare requires.

But within that trusted boundary, we're still trying to get everyone to pre-agree on every implementation detail.

What if there's a middle path?

Take "Kill the Clipboard" - everyone agrees patients shouldn't fill out paper forms at every appointment. The outcome is clear. But right now, we're waiting for every network to implement patient intake identically before any data can flow.

What if each CMS Aligned Network published discoverable, machine-readable specifications for their capabilities? Apps integrating with any network would have a clear map of requirements. Networks publishing cleaner, more adoptable specs would naturally attract more integrations. The coordination problem becomes a discovery problem.

The governance layer stays intact - TEFCA's trust framework, BAAs, safety rails. But the innovation layer becomes more fluid. Instead of 18-month committee cycles to perfect specifications, networks publish their approach and let the market signal what works.

I'm not suggesting we abandon safety protections. But maybe we're spending enormous energy perfecting specifications when we should be perfecting the mechanism for proposing specifications.

The particulars of the spec might matter less than agreeing on how to publish one.
