## The Game Has Changed

My career has been anything but linear. It started on the football field, briefly as a lineman for the Arizona Cardinals and, somehow, I find myself running sales for a healthcare technology consultancy. The connection isn't obvious until you realize both jobs require the same skill: reading the defense.

In football, you don't run the same play against every defense. You read the formation, identify the pressure, and adapt. So why, in sales, are so many of us still running plays from a bygone era against a defense we've never seen before?

My approach to both has been the same: take individual occurrences, identify the pattern, and build a set of operating rules that work consistently. On the field, that meant studying film and recognizing defensive schemes. In sales, it means diagnosing why deals stall and building frameworks to get them unstuck.

For the better part of the last year, I've been obsessed with the dominant outcome of the modern B2B sales playbook: **'no decision.'** It's the ghost deal. The budget was there, the champion was real, but the deal died from a thousand internal papercuts.

And it's not just me. Research analyzing over 2.5 million sales conversations confirms that [**40% to 60% of B2B deals end in "no decision"**](https://challengerinc.com/blog/how-sales-leaders-can-unlock-seller-performance-in-an-uncertain-economy/)—and it's getting worse. Win rates have [dropped 27% since 2021](https://www.repvue.com/cloud-index/2024/Q4). Sales cycles are [25% longer](https://www.cognism.com/blog/ultimate-guide-to-the-b2b-buyers-journey). A staggering [69% of sales reps missed quota in 2024](https://valuecore.ai/blog/value-based-selling-what-it-is-and-why-its-critical-for-sales-success-in-2025/).

This isn't a failure of skill; it's a failure to read the field. The playbook you need is determined by the game you're in. The problem is, **the game has changed**, and we're still missing the theory to explain how.

I believe I've stumbled across the right lens for writing the right playbook: [**Transaction Cost Economics**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transaction_cost) **(TCE)**.

My central argument: **Every B2B sales methodology in history has, consciously or not, been an attempt to lower the customer's transaction costs.** The job has always been to make it easier to buy. But the specific costs—whether finding the right information, building internal consensus, or managing a complex implementation—have shifted dramatically over time.

What worked in a nascent market with high search costs (Sales-Led Growth) is relatively useless in an efficient market dominated by frictionless evaluation (Product-Led Growth). And today, in a saturated and complex market, the enemy has changed again. The winner is the one who can drive down the crippling internal costs of consensus and implementation.

Over the past year, I've developed a new tool—a **potential well curve**—that maps these transaction costs to market state, allowing you to diagnose your market, understand the dominant costs your buyers face, and finally, deploy the right GTM playbook.

## The Hidden Costs of Doing Business: A History of Transaction Cost Economics

Why do companies exist?

It's a question so fundamental it sounds almost absurd. We're so surrounded by firms—from the corner coffee shop to the multinational tech conglomerate—that we take their existence for granted. But in the 1930s, a young British economist named [Ronald Coase](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1991/coase/facts/) asked this question and found the answer so profound it would win him a Nobel Prize half a century later.

At the time, the reigning economic theory had no good answer. It treated firms as simple "black boxes" that magically transformed labor and capital into products, but it couldn't explain *why* these boxes were the dominant structure for organizing economic activity. Why wasn't the entire economy just a swarm of independent contractors, constantly buying and selling services from one another in a perfectly free market?

**Coase's revolutionary insight**, published in his 1937 essay "[The Nature of the Firm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nature_of_the_Firm)," was that **using the market has a cost**. This idea, which now seems obvious, was a tectonic shift in economic thought. He argued that for any transaction to take place on the open market, there are hidden costs of participation—the "costs of using the price mechanism":

* The cost of **discovering** the relevant prices  
* The cost of **negotiating** a contract for each individual exchange  
* The cost of **enforcing** that contract and ensuring the work is done to standard

Firms, Coase argued, are the rational response to these costs. It's often far cheaper and more efficient to hire an employee and direct their work than to constantly go out to the open market to contract for every single task. A company will expand, absorbing activities, until the cost of organizing one more task inside the firm becomes equal to the cost of buying that same task on the open market. **The firm is a carefully constructed island of managerial control in the vast ocean of the free market, designed to do one thing: minimize transaction costs.**

Coase's idea was a seismic event, but for several decades it remained a brilliant but largely academic concept. The intellectual earthquake had happened, but no one had yet drawn the new maps. That task fell to another future Nobel laureate, [**Oliver Williamson**](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/williamson/facts/), who in the 1970s and 80s took Coase's foundational insight and operationalized it, building the framework that is now widely known as Transaction Cost Economics. If Coase opened the door, Williamson walked through it and furnished the room.

Williamson's central focus was on the contracts—both explicit and implicit—that govern economic relationships. He started from a more cynical (or perhaps realistic) view of human nature, introducing two critical behavioral assumptions:

1. [**Bounded Rationality**](https://www.behavioraleconomics.com/resources/mini-encyclopedia-of-be/bounded-rationality/)**:** Unlike the "perfectly rational" actors in classical economic models, real humans are limited in their ability to process information and predict the future. We can't possibly foresee every contingency, which means every long-term contract is, by definition, incomplete. There will always be gaps.  
2. **Opportunism:** Williamson defined this as "self-interest seeking with guile." People might exploit gaps in contracts for their own benefit, creating risk and uncertainty in any transaction. (In plain English: people will game the system if you let them. Anyone who's negotiated a complex B2B contract knows this intimately—which is why buying committees grow larger and legal reviews get longer.)

With these assumptions in place, Williamson argued that the goal of a contract, and of a firm's structure, is to safeguard against the risks that arise from incomplete agreements. He identified a key factor that determined the level of this risk: [**asset specificity**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asset_specificity).

This concept is crucial for understanding modern B2B sales. Asset specificity refers to how much an investment is tied to a specific relationship. If a supplier buys a generic truck to deliver goods, that truck has low asset specificity; it can be easily redeployed to serve another customer. But if that supplier builds a custom pipeline to a specific chemical plant, that pipeline has extremely high asset specificity. Its value is almost entirely dependent on the survival of that single relationship.

When asset specificity is high, the parties become locked into a dependent relationship. This is where the risks of bounded rationality and opportunism become magnified. Williamson's work provided a framework for analyzing these risks and structuring "governance" mechanisms—whether a simple contract or a full-blown merger—to manage them effectively.

### The Modern Application: From Firm Boundaries to Sales Strategy

So, what does the arcane world of firm boundaries and asset specificity have to do with your stalled deal?

Everything.

While Coase and Williamson used TCE to explain the boundaries of a firm—the "make-or-buy" decision—we can apply the exact same logic to understand the **boundaries of a deal**. For a modern B2B buyer, the choice is rarely between buying your solution and buying a competitor's. The real battle is between buying your solution and doing nothing at all.

It's a "buy-or-do-nothing" decision, and the outcome is determined by the same force: **transaction costs**.

The buyer isn't just evaluating your product; they're subconsciously calculating the immense internal costs required to get a deal done and successfully implemented. Williamson's framework gives us the perfect language to categorize these costs in a sales context:

* **Search Costs:** The initial cost of navigating a crowded market to find and vet potential solutions. In a world with a thousand SaaS vendors for every problem, this is the cost of information overload. *(Remember Coase's "cost of discovering the relevant prices"? In 2024, the problem isn't finding information—it's drowning in it.)*  
* **Consensus Costs:** This is the internal, political cost of navigating the buying committee. It's the effort required to get a dozen (or more) stakeholders with competing priorities to agree on a single path forward. It's the modern-day embodiment of Williamson's "bounded rationality" and "opportunism," as individual department heads act in their own self-interest.  
* **Implementation Costs:** This is the modern SaaS equivalent of "asset specificity." Integrating a new, complex tool into a company's unique tech stack is a highly specific investment. It requires significant resources, time, and political capital. The fear of this post-contract friction is often the single biggest killer of a deal.

Here's the data point that makes this real: [**77% of B2B buyers**](https://www.gartner.com/en/sales/insights/b2b-buying-journey) **now describe their most recent purchase as "very complex or difficult."** They're not complaining about finding vendors or comparing features. They're talking about the internal nightmare of actually getting something done.

From this perspective, the "no decision" outcome is no longer a mystery. **It is a perfectly rational economic choice.** When the perceived internal transaction costs of consensus and implementation are higher than the perceived value of the solution, the most logical decision for the customer is to do nothing.

The job of a modern growth team, therefore, is not just to sell a product. It is to be a **Transaction Cost Economist** for the buyer, actively working to lower these three internal costs and clear the path for a deal.

With this new lens, we can now look back at the history of sales not as a random collection of tactics, but as a clear, evolutionary response to the shifting landscape of transaction costs.

## The Market Curve: Mapping the History of B2B Sales

Now that we have our economic lens, we can look at the history of B2B sales in a new light. It's not a random series of trends, but a logical progression along a predictable curve. To visualize this journey, I've developed a new tool: the **Potential Well of Transaction Costs**.

This graph maps the relationship between **Market Complexity** (on the x-axis) and **Transaction Costs** (on the y-axis). It shows how, as markets develop, they move through three distinct phases:

1. **The Nascent Market:** In the beginning, when a new category is created, the market is undefined. There are few vendors and little public information. This creates incredibly high transaction costs, specifically **high search costs**. Buyers don't even know what to look for, let alone how to find it.  
2. **The Efficient Market:** As the market matures, more vendors enter, competition increases, and information becomes widely available. Best practices are established, and products become easier to compare. This drives transaction costs down to their lowest point, creating an efficient, low-friction environment.  
3. **The Saturated & Complex Market:** Eventually, the market becomes flooded with vendors. Products become increasingly complex and specialized, leading to a paradox of choice. The sheer volume of options and the difficulty of integrating new solutions cause transaction costs to skyrocket again, this time driven by overwhelming **consensus** and **implementation** costs.

The story of B2B sales over the last 70 years is the story of GTM teams developing new playbooks to conquer the dominant transaction costs of each phase. Let's walk the curve.

---

### The Nascent Market (1950s-1980s): Conquering Search Costs with Sales-Led Growth

Imagine trying to buy a new piece of office equipment in 1965\. There is no internet, no G2 or Gartner, no peer reviews. The market is a black box.

If you wanted product specs, you literally had to call a company and wait 6-8 weeks for them to mail you a brochure. The single greatest barrier to a sale—the highest transaction cost by far—was the **cost of search**. The buyer's primary challenge was simply finding a viable solution and gathering enough information to make a decision.

This market reality gave rise to the first dominant GTM motion: **Sales-Led Growth (SLG)**. The salesperson was the market. They were the search engine, the product expert, and the source of truth all rolled into one.

Methodologies from this era—from Dale Carnegie's relationship-building to early consultative techniques—were all weapons designed to do one thing: **lower the customer's search costs**. Whether through persuasion, relationship-building, or information delivery, the seller's job was to be the bridge between the buyer and the solution they needed.

In this game, the salesperson was the star quarterback. The strategy was to put the ball in the hands of a charismatic individual who could single-handedly carry the information, build trust, and guide the buyer through an otherwise opaque process. The entire GTM model was built to solve for a lack of information, making the assertive, knowledgeable seller the most valuable player on the field.

Let's continue our journey to the bottom of the well.

---

### The Efficient Market (1990s-2010s): Lowering Evaluation Costs with Product-Led Growth

As the nascent market matured, a new force emerged that would fundamentally reshape the economic landscape: **the internet**. The arrival of the web browser in the mid-90s triggered a Cambrian explosion of information. Suddenly, the black box was blasted open. Buyers could now research vendors, compare features, and read early reviews with a few clicks. The scarcity of information that defined the previous era evaporated, and with it, the high search costs.

This plunged the market down into the bottom of the potential well, creating a phase of incredible efficiency. With information now a commodity, the primary transaction cost shifted from *search* to *evaluation*. The new question wasn't "Can I find a solution?" but "How do I choose the right solution from these twenty viable options without spending months in meetings?"

This new reality demanded a new GTM motion, one built for a world where buyers wanted to self-educate and try before they buy. This was the dawn of **Product-Led Growth (PLG)**. The strategy was simple and revolutionary: make the product itself the primary driver of customer acquisition, conversion, and expansion. The rise of freemium and free trial models was the ultimate expression of lowering evaluation costs. The most effective way to help a customer choose was to let them experience the value firsthand, friction-free.

The role of the salesperson had to evolve. They were no longer the gatekeeper of information. Methodologies like [SPIN Selling](https://www.pipedrive.com/en/blog/spin-selling) and [Solution Selling](https://books.google.com/books/about/Solution_Selling_Creating_Buyers_in_Diff.html?id=9AAKAQAAMAAJ) emerged as the new playbook. The goal was no longer to persuade, but to diagnose. A great salesperson in the efficient market was a consultant, an expert problem-solver who could help a customer navigate the evaluation process, understand their own needs more deeply, and build a business case for the best solution—which, thanks to PLG, the customer had often already tried.

For a time, this was a golden era. Transaction costs were at an all-time low. The synergy between educated buyers, product-led GTM, and consultative selling created a highly efficient market.

But this efficiency contained the seeds of its own destruction. The very forces that drove costs down—the proliferation of information and the explosion of new vendors—would eventually push the market past a tipping point, creating a new kind of complexity and sending transaction costs climbing once again.

We've reached the inflection point. We've journeyed to the bottom of the well, the golden era of efficiency. Now, we climb out the other side, into the chaos of the modern market. This is the part of the story that everyone in B2B sales is living through right now. It's where the old playbooks break, and the pain begins.

---

### The Saturated & Complex Market (Today): The Revenge of Transaction Costs

An era of efficiency can quickly become a victim of its own success. The very forces that drove transaction costs to an all-time low—unlimited information and endless vendors—eventually reached a tipping point and began to work against the buyer. The market became saturated. The solutions became radically more complex and interconnected. The result? A new, brutal climb up the right side of the potential well.

We are now in the **Saturated & Complex Market**. Transaction costs are skyrocketing again, but this time they aren't driven by a scarcity of information. They are driven by an overwhelming abundance of it. This has created two new, formidable enemies for any B2B deal:

#### 1\. Crippling Consensus Costs

The modern buying committee has ballooned to an [average of 11 stakeholders](https://corporatevisions.com/blog/b2b-buying-behavior-statistics-trends/), with some reaching over 20\. But the challenge isn't just the number—it's the complexity. Here's what you're actually up against:

* [**89% of purchases**](https://www.thedrum.com/open-mic/the-b2b-buying-committee-is-real-heres-how-to-win-them-over) **involve two or more departments**, each with their own success metrics and concerns  
* [**52% of buying committees**](https://thunderbit.com/blog/b2b-buying-stats) **include VP-level or higher decision makers**, shifting the conversation from features to strategic business impact  
* **The CFO is now personally involved in [79% of purchases](https://www.prezentor.com/why-the-cfo-matters/)**, making a rigorous ROI case non-negotiable  
* **Legal and compliance teams have the power to slow or block deals in [61% of cases](https://sopro.io/resources/blog/b2b-buyer-statistics-and-insights/)**, adding yet another layer of scrutiny

And here's the kicker: [**78% of sales reps**](https://www.mediafly.com/evolved-selling-community/metrics-matter/) **identify "dealing with multiple stakeholders with different agendas" as their single biggest challenge**. [Over 40% of B2B deals](https://www.marketing-interactive.com/report-over-40-of-b2b-deals-stall-due-to-hidden-buyers) now stall specifically because of this internal misalignment.

In healthcare, this problem is particularly acute. When a hospital system evaluates a new Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS), the buying committee needs to satisfy not just the typical cast of IT, finance, and legal—but also 41 faculty radiologists across 10 clinical sections, 253 technologists, and multiple departments of end-users. One recent PACS selection process defined 11 distinct evaluation pillars and 236 specific criteria just to compare vendors. The cost of running that gauntlet, in time and political capital alone, is staggering. Many vendors simply walk away.

Each person brings their own priorities, fears, and departmental biases. The cost of wrangling these disparate groups, building a unified business case, and navigating the internal politics has become the single greatest barrier to a decision.

#### 2\. Crushing Implementation Costs

In a world of complex, interconnected tech stacks, a new piece of software isn't a standalone tool; it's a major integration project. The buyer's fear is no longer "Will this product work?" but "What will break when we plug this in?"

The fear isn't hypothetical. [WellSpan Health's "Project One"](https://www.e4.health/overcoming-the-challenge-of-interfacing-5-ehrs-with-epic/)—an effort to consolidate five legacy EHRs into a single Epic platform—carried an estimated cost of **$188.7 million over three years**. The implementation resulted in failed data interfaces, duplicate patient records, and the need for extensive manual data cleanup. When a botched implementation can cost nine figures and compromise patient safety, "no decision" starts looking like the prudent choice.

The cost of implementation—measured in engineering hours, change management, and political capital—is a massive, often unspoken, hurdle. This is Williamson's "asset specificity" in its purest form. The investment required to integrate your solution into their unique environment is highly specific and largely unrecoverable if it fails. This high asset specificity creates lock-in risk, which drives hesitation and, ultimately, that cursed "no decision."

## The Market's Reflexive Loop

The climb into the Saturated & Complex Market wasn't an accident. It was a predictable, reflexive reaction to the successes of the past. The market, in its relentless pursuit of efficiency, created a feedback loop where the solution to one problem became the root cause of the next, more complex challenge.

#### Force 1: The SaaS Paradox

The foundational problem of the nascent market was information scarcity. The solution, born of the internet and cloud computing, was the **SaaS explosion**: a world of infinite, accessible, and specialized software. This solved the search cost problem brilliantly. But in doing so, it changed the fundamental economics of B2B.

The shift to a subscription model meant the true value of a customer was no longer in the initial sale, but in their **Net Revenue Retention (NRR)**. However, the GTM machine failed to adapt. Sales incentives remained heavily skewed towards the upfront close. This created a profound misalignment: **sales teams were rewarded for acquiring customers, not for delivering outcomes**.

Think about what this incentive structure encouraged: close any deal you can, hit your quota, collect your commission, and move on. The long-term success of that customer? That's someone else's problem.

This misalignment encouraged the proliferation of "band-aid" solutions—tools that were easy to sell but difficult to implement and prove value from. The result is the pain every leader feels today: a bloated, expensive, and under-utilized tech stack where it's nearly impossible to assess the true impact of any single tool.

#### Force 2: The Committee as a Corrective Measure

The modern cross-functional buying committee is a direct, rational response to this SaaS paradox. Leadership, burned by years of purchasing tools that failed to deliver value, created an internal system of checks and balances. If a new piece of software was going to impact multiple departments, it seemed logical to give each of them a veto.

This attempt at risk management, however, has become the single greatest source of friction in the buying process. The committee's primary function is no longer to select the best solution, but to **shield the organization from the risk of another bad decision**. Given the complexity and the number of stakeholders involved, the safest and most politically expedient choice is almost always to maintain the status quo.

**This is the brutal feedback loop:** The SaaS model's misaligned incentives led to poor purchasing decisions, which led to the creation of the buying committee, whose very structure now makes it nearly impossible to make any purchasing decision at all. These twin forces have sent consensus and implementation costs into the stratosphere.

The playbooks of the past are fundamentally ill-equipped for this new reality.

Sales-Led Growth, designed for a world of information scarcity, feels tone-deaf. The buyer doesn't need you to tell them what's out there; they need you to help them navigate what they've already found.

Product-Led Growth, designed for frictionless evaluation, fails to address the massive post-purchase friction that buyers now fear most. A free trial is great for letting someone kick the tires, but it does nothing to help them build consensus across 13 stakeholders or de-risk a complex implementation.

Even [**The Challenger Sale**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Challenger_Sale), which sought to teach the customer something new, can backfire if that new insight further complicates an already overwhelming decision. Teaching a buyer about a problem they didn't know they had is only valuable if you can also show them how to solve it without blowing up their organization.

For many industries, the game has changed. The dominant transaction costs are no longer outside the building; they are inside. The biggest challenges are internal consensus and implementation. This new defensive formation requires a new offensive playbook. It requires a new GTM motion, one designed not just to sell the product, but to shepherd the entire organization through the complexity of the purchase and the pain of implementation.

This is the era of **Implementation-Led Growth (ILG)**.

### The Implementation-Led Growth (ILG) Playbook

If the core problem is a systemic failure to translate a chosen solution into organizational value, then we need a GTM motion designed specifically to solve that problem. This is **Implementation-Led Growth (ILG)**. It redefines the objective: the goal is not to sell software, but to deliver a successful implementation that drives business outcomes.

This is not a novel idea. It's the practical application of a formal, evidence-based field called [**Implementation Science**](https://impsciuw.org/implementation-science/learn/implementation-science-overview/). Born from the high-stakes world of healthcare, this discipline provides a roadmap for how to successfully integrate a proven "thing" (the Evidence) into a complex human system (the Context) through active Facilitation.

The [PARIHS framework](https://www.nccmt.ca/resources/search/85) from this field gives us a powerful equation:

**Successful Implementation \= f(Evidence, Context, Facilitation)**

The ILG playbook is a set of tools designed to systematically manage these three variables.

#### E is for Evidence: Establishing Credibility

Before any discussion of implementation can begin, the solution itself must be credible. "Evidence" refers to the strength and quality of the data and user experience backing the intervention. It is the answer to the buyer's fundamental question: **"Why should we believe this works?"**

This is established through:

* **Clinical and Efficacy Data:** Performance benchmarks, security audits, peer-reviewed data, or formal clinical trial results  
* **Robust Case Studies:** Demonstrations of clear, quantifiable ROI from existing, similar customers  
* **End-User Experience:** Data on user satisfaction, adoption rates, and workflow improvements

This isn't new. Value-based selling has been preaching this for years. But here's the difference: in ILG, this evidence isn't just a sales tool—it's the foundation for everything that comes next. Without credible evidence, you have no business moving forward.

#### C is for Context: The Diagnostic Phase

With the credibility of the Evidence established, the focus shifts to the buyer's world. The Implementation Scientist's most critical job is to become an expert on the buyer's internal landscape, a process captured in the **Contextual Blueprint**. This diagnostic replaces superficial qualification methods (like BANT) with a rigorous assessment of:

* **The Inner Setting:** The structural, political, and cultural realities of the organization. Who holds informal power? How do budgets *really* get approved? What are the sacred cows you can't touch?  
* **The Outer Setting:** The external forces shaping the urgency and constraints of the decision. Is there a regulatory deadline forcing action? A competitive threat changing the political calculus? Budget cycles or leadership changes on the horizon? These external pressures matter primarily because they influence internal stakeholder behavior and motivation.  
* **The Individuals Involved:** A deep analysis of the key stakeholders to understand their individual **Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation**. Who is the champion, who is the blocker, and what is the root cause of their resistance? How are they personally affected by the external pressures in the Outer Setting?

This is a **risk assessment**. A deal should be qualified out if the Contextual Blueprint reveals an environment hostile to a successful implementation. If the political landscape is a minefield and your champion has no real power, no amount of product polish will save you.

#### F is for Facilitation: The Path to Success

With strong Evidence and a deep understanding of the Context, the final variable is active **Facilitation**. This is the process of guiding the buying committee to a shared plan. The key deliverables are:

**The Mutual Implementation Plan (Replaces the Proposal):**

A traditional proposal is a document of "what we will sell you." A **Mutual Implementation Plan** is a co-authored project plan detailing the timeline, resources from both parties, and KPIs for success *before* the contract is signed.

This transforms the terrifying unknown of implementation into a manageable project. It answers the buyer's unspoken fear: "What happens after we sign?" Instead of ambiguity and risk, they see a clear roadmap with shared accountability.

**"Red Team" Workshops (Replaces the Demo):**

A standard demo shows off features. A **Red Team workshop** is designed to stress-test the implementation plan. It's a facilitated meeting with key technical stakeholders to actively probe for the biggest risks.

"What could go wrong with our data migration?"  
 "Where are the potential integration bottlenecks?"  
 "Who on your team is going to push back on this workflow change?"

This builds incredible trust. You're not selling them a perfect vision; you're helping them identify landmines while there's still time to navigate around them. This directly attacks implementation fear.

### The New Incentives: Rewarding the Right Behavior

Behavior follows incentives. An ILG model only works if the GTM team is rewarded for the same thing the customer wants: **a successful implementation that drives outcomes**.

This means evolving the compensation plan. While the traditional closed deal must remain a component, a significant portion of a rep's variable compensation (perhaps **25-40%**) should be tied to post-sale outcomes:

* **Successful Onboarding:** Commission is paid out in full only after the customer has been successfully onboarded, as defined by the Mutual Implementation Plan  
* **Customer Health Score:** A kicker is tied to the customer achieving a "green" health score within their first 90 or 180 days  
* **First-Year Net Revenue Retention (NRR):** The ultimate measure of success. This ensures the team is focused on finding the right long-term partners, not just closing a quick deal

This isn't radical—it's where the market is already heading. [**80% of sales leaders**](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/net-revenue-retention/) **now say they prioritize long-term customer relationships over short-term wins**, and leading SaaS companies are already tying compensation directly to Net Revenue Retention.

This new model requires more work. It's more consultative, more strategic, and demands a deeper understanding of the customer's business. But **it's the only playbook designed to win in a market where the biggest competitor is "no decision."** It aligns the seller with the buyer against their common enemy: internal complexity.

When a salesperson's income is materially dependent on the customer's success one year after the close, their behavior during the sales cycle fundamentally changes. They're no longer incentivized to close any deal through persuasion and pressure. Instead, they're financially motivated to close only the *right* deals with customers who have a high probability of successful implementation and value realization.

## Comparing Approaches: How GTM Motions Stack

Each GTM motion evolved to solve the dominant transaction cost of its era. They don't replace each other—they layer.

|  | SLG | PLG | ILG |
| :---- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Primary Transaction Cost Solved** | Search Costs (finding information) | Evaluation Costs (comparing options) | Consensus & Implementation Costs |
| **Market Context** | Nascent (information scarce) | Efficient (information abundant) | Saturated & Complex (information overwhelming) |
| **Buyer's Main Question** | "What solutions exist?" | "Does this actually work?" | "Can we make this work *here*?" |
| **Sales Team's Role** | Information broker and persuader | Product educator and consultant | Implementation facilitator and risk mitigator |
| **Key Deliverable** | Persuasive pitch and proposal | Free trial or demo \+ ROI case | Mutual Implementation Plan |
| **Success Metric** | Closed deals | Product adoption and expansion | Customer health score and first-year NRR |

**The evolution isn't about replacement—it's about accumulation.** You still need the relationship-building instincts of SLG. You still need the product excellence and self-service efficiency of PLG. But if you're operating in a saturated, complex market (and most B2B software is), you need something more.

You need to actively de-risk the implementation. You need to facilitate internal consensus. You need to co-author the path to value *before* the contract is signed.

That's ILG. It's not a rejection of what came before—it's the necessary next layer when the market has evolved past the point where features and free trials are sufficient to overcome inertia.

### Conclusion: Reading the Field and Calling the Right Play

The journey from the sales floors of the 1950s to today's Zoom calls isn't random—it maps perfectly to Transaction Cost Economics. The dominant playbooks of each era attacked the transaction costs that mattered most at the time.

Today, if you're seeing deals stall despite strong interest, it's likely because you're still running plays designed for a different defense. The "no decision" outcome isn't irrational—it's a calculated response to internal complexity that your current motion doesn't address.

So here's what I'd recommend:

**Run a transaction cost audit on your pipeline.** Look at your last 10 stalled deals. How many died because of:

* Lack of awareness or information? (Search costs—rare today)  
* Inability to prove value or differentiate? (Evaluation costs—PLG solves this)
